Find It & Tag ItEquipment identification walk — 117 items

You have read about this equipment. Now go and find it.

Working one system at a time, locate every item listed, record it, and write down where it is and one fact you can only get by standing in front of it. When a section is complete, walk it with your supervisor — they check your work, ask you what things do, and sign it off.

Not everything here can have a tag hung on it — a compressor sits inside a machine, a drive sits inside a cabinet, a coil sits inside an AHU. Every row tells you how to record that one. It is not a test you can fail — it is a record of what you have been shown.

00

How this works — read before you start

Safety first — this is a LOOK exercise, not a TOUCH exercise

  1. Open nothing. No electrical panels, no ATS doors, no switchgear, no gas trains, no boiler doors, no drive cabinets.
  2. Operate nothing. Do not push buttons, turn valves, flip switches, or press a single reset. Finding a valve is the exercise; turning it is not.
  3. Go escorted anywhere your supervisor says escorted — roofs, high-voltage rooms, confined spaces, lab areas, anywhere needing a permit.
  4. Wear what the area requires and follow every posted sign.
  5. If you cannot find something, leave it blank and ask. A blank line is a good question. A guess written down becomes a wrong answer somebody trusts later.
  6. Stop and report immediately for a gas smell, a CO alarm, water where it should not be, a machine that sounds wrong, or anything that scares you. Do not finish the section first.

If it cannot be tagged, how does anyone know you found it?

This is the fair question, and the sheet answers it three ways. A photograph on its own is not proof — one drive cabinet looks like another, and a description can be copied off a training page.

TAG ITAccessible and safe to mark. Hang the numbered tag on it. The tag is the proof.
TAG THE ACCESSThe device itself is inside something — a compressor, a fan, a coil, a duct sensor. Tag the panel, door, housing or duct you would open to reach it, with the same number and the word inside. Now there is a physical marker at the closest point a person can legitimately stand.
READ ONLYThe door stays closed. Switchgear, ATS, drive cabinets, control panels. Nothing gets a tag — the label you copy down is the proof, and reading a label without opening a door is the skill being taught.
VERIFYNot a component — a condition to confirm and report on.

Then the column that actually settles it — “Proof you were there.” Every row names something you can only get by standing in front of the equipment: a serial number, a label date, a meter reading, a section count, a point name, the position a switch is in right now. Copy it off the metal, not off this sheet. A serial number and a label date belong to one piece of equipment and nothing else.

Photographs help, but frame them as locators: the device and a fixed reference in the same shot — an equipment nameplate, a room number, or the nearest tag. A close-up of a gauge proves nothing. That same gauge with tag B-19 in the corner proves position.

And the one that really settles it: your supervisor walks the section and says “show me C-03.” No paperwork survives that. Everything above is what makes the walk quick — the walk is the verification.

The second badge — what it is for

This is the one that turns a list into understanding. Sort your thinking by it and equipment stops being a hundred separate objects.

MoveMakes something happen — pumps, fans, compressors, burners.
CarryHolds or transfers — pipe, duct, coils, barrels, tanks, flue.
ControlDecides — panels, controllers, drives, control valves.
ProveTells you it really happened — flow switches, current switches, end switches, flame scanners. Command is not proof.
ProtectActs whether or not anyone agrees — cutouts, limits, relief valves, freezestats. Mostly hardwired, mostly manual reset.
IsolateHand valves and shutoffs. Record the normal position of every one.

Every row also carries a red line: what goes wrong when that item is wrong. Read it as you go. It is the difference between knowing where the condenser-water isolation valve is, and knowing that left shut it means no CW flow and the chiller will not start. The second sentence is the one you use on a call.

A good way to walk it: do one group at a time across a system rather than working top to bottom. Tag every Protect item on the boiler in one pass and you will understand that boiler better than tagging all twenty-four in list order.

One phrase worth understanding before you walk a boiler room

Several rows say a room can “go negative.” It means the air pressure in that room has dropped below the pressure outside it — because exhaust fans, an air handler, a compressor, or an air unit that is switched off are pulling more air out than is coming in.

A flue works on draft: hot gas rising, carrying combustion products up and out. If the room goes negative, the room is pulling harder than the flue is lifting — so the flue becomes the easiest place for the room to draw air from, and flue gas comes back down into the room. That is called spillage, and the barometric damper is usually where it happens, because it is a deliberate opening in the flue.

At the same time, a burner short of combustion air burns rich and starts making carbon monoxide of its own. So you get flue gas entering the room, and that flue gas is unusually high in CO.

None of it announces itself. Carbon monoxide has no smell, no colour and no warning. The fire keeps burning and the panel looks perfect. That is why the combustion-air interlock holds the boilers off rather than simply raising an alarm — and why nobody ever jumpers an air-unit proof to get heat back on a cold night.

About the valves

  1. Record the normal position of every manual valve — open, closed, throttled. That record is worth more than the tag: if nobody knows what normal looks like, nobody can spot wrong.
  2. Some valves must never be closed. A relief discharge is never valved. Nothing may isolate a low-water cutoff. Sprinkler control valves are supervised open. If you find a valve that should not exist, that is a real finding — report it.
  3. An idle machine is not an isolated machine. A leaking or open isolation valve on an off chiller or an idle tower cell keeps passing water — wasted pumping and blended temperatures, with nothing alarming.
  4. Auto valves have position feedback for a reason. An actuator can stroke while the disc stays put. That is why a sequence waits on an end switch, not on a command.
  5. Never close a valve you do not understand — and on this exercise, never close one at all.

For the supervisor signing this off

  1. Walk the section and say “show me” for three items you pick — including at least one TAG THE ACCESS row, because those are the ones paperwork cannot prove.
  2. Check the proof column against the equipment. A serial number, a label date or a meter reading either matches the metal or it does not.
  3. On valve rows ask “what would happen if this one were shut?”
  4. On Prove rows ask “what does the BMS show if this device fails but the equipment is fine?”
  5. Sign only when you would be comfortable with them describing that equipment on a phone call to you at 2am.

Fault index — when something goes wrong, which tags matter

This is the pay-off. Once the tags are up, a symptom points at a short list of things to go and look at — instead of a whole building.

Work down each list in order; it is roughly the order of likelihood. None of this replaces reading the BMS and the machine's own screen first — it tells you where to walk once you have.

Add your own rows. Every time you work a real call, write the symptom and the tags that turned out to matter. In a year this becomes the most useful page you own — because it is yours, and it is about this building.

When every section is signed

Hand the completed sheet to your manager. Keep a copy.

Then do the thing that makes it stick: pick any three items and trace what connects them. Follow water from a boiler through its isolation valves to a coil and back. Follow the wire from a current switch to a controller to a graphic. Follow air from a louver to a burner. The tags teach you where things are — tracing between them teaches you how the building actually works.
