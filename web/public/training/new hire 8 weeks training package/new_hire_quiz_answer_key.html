Multiple choice — with explanations

Q1Why does a lab building run 100% outside air instead of recirculating?C
  • ARecirculating wears out the fans
  • BCode requires it in all offices too
  • CLab air may carry fumes and hazards, so it must go out, not around
  • DIt is cheaper to condition

WhyLab air can never be recirculated — it is conditioned once, used once, and exhausted. That is what makes lab HVAC expensive and why the ERM matters.

Q2Which piece of equipment is where the water side and the air side meet?D
  • ALEF
  • BCooling tower
  • CBoiler
  • DAHU

WhyThe AHU holds the chilled-water and hot-water coils and pushes the air — every loop and the air path meet there.

Q3What do the CW, CHW, and HW pumps actually do?C
  • AFilter the water
  • BCool the water
  • CMake the loops circulate — no pump, no flow, no cooling or heat
  • DAdd chemicals

WhyPumps are the movers. Water only picks up or drops off heat if it is flowing.

Q4The condenser water loop runs between which two pieces?A
  • ACooling tower and chiller
  • BChiller and AHU
  • CAHU and LEF
  • DBoiler and VAV

WhyThe CW pump shuttles water tower ⇄ chiller: the chiller dumps building heat into it, the tower evaporates it away outside.

Q5When it is cold outside, free cooling means…D
  • AThe boiler pre-cools the water
  • BThe chiller runs faster
  • COpening the windows
  • DTower water cools the chilled loop through a plate heat exchanger — compressors off

WhyCold outdoor air makes the tower water cold enough on its own; the free-cooling pump and HX chill the CHW loop directly. Big winter energy savings.

Q6Our ERM recovers energy using…B
  • AMixing the two airstreams
  • BGlycol run-around coils — a coil in each airstream tied by a pumped glycol loop
  • CElectric preheat
  • DA rotating heat wheel

WhyA coil in the exhaust and a coil in the OA, connected by pumped glycol — heat crosses over but the airstreams never touch, so no cross-contamination.

Q7Typical chilled-water supply temperature is around…C
  • A70°F
  • B58°F
  • C44°F
  • D34°F

WhyAbout 44°F is the classic CHW supply ballpark — always confirm the actual setpoint on the plant.

Q8A hot-water loop is running 125°F supply. What must you know before calling it a problem?B
  • AThe chilled water setpoint
  • BWhether the plant is a condensing boiler (runs cool on purpose) or non-condensing (needs hot return)
  • CThe tower blowdown rate
  • DThe building’s occupancy

WhyCondensing boilers want ~120–140°F supply and cold return to condense flue gas. Non-condensing boilers want ~160–180°F and return above ~140°F, or the heat exchanger corrodes. Same reading, opposite verdicts.

Q9A VAV/FPT reheat coil gets its heat from…B
  • AThe exhaust air
  • BThe boiler’s hot water loop
  • CElectric strips always
  • DThe condenser water

WhyReheat coils ride the heating loop — the boiler makes the hot water, the HW pump delivers it.

Q10In the WSHP setup, what trims the common loop temperature?B
  • AThe MAU
  • BThe fluid cooler / tower and the boiler
  • CThe chiller
  • DEach heat pump individually

WhyZone heat pumps push and pull heat on the loop; the cooler rejects excess and the boiler adds heat only if the loop runs short.

Q11In a WSHP building, fresh air for the zones comes from…D
  • AThe heat pumps themselves
  • BThe stairwells
  • COpening dampers on each WSHP
  • DA separate HV / MAU, with exhaust taking it back out

WhyWSHPs only recirculate zone air for temperature. The make-up air unit brings in and tempers the outside air the exhaust removes.

Q12Used lab air leaves the building through…C
  • AThe AHU return
  • BThe cooling tower
  • CThe LEF, discharged high above the roof
  • DRelief louvers at grade

WhyLab exhaust fans throw the air high above the roof so it disperses and never re-enters an intake.

Q13CRAC units cool equipment rooms using…D
  • AOutside air only
  • BCondenser water
  • CRefrigerant from the chiller directly
  • DA chilled-water coil

WhyCRACs on this campus ride the chilled-water loop, same as the AHU coil — just dedicated to IT/equipment rooms.

Q14A GEF (general exhaust fan) serves…A
  • ARestrooms, electrical/MDF rooms, elevator machine rooms and mechanical spaces — straight to the roof
  • BFume hoods and lab benches
  • CThe cooling tower basin
  • DThe AHU return air

WhyGeneral exhaust clears odor and heat from support spaces. It never passes through the ERM or the LEF — that air is neither hazardous nor worth recovering heat from.

Q15In a 100% outside-air lab building, what sets the supply airflow?C
  • AThe outdoor air temperature
  • BThe thermostat in the corridor
  • CThe total exhaust — LEF plus GEF plus exfiltration; supply replaces what exhaust removes
  • DThe chilled water setpoint

WhyMass balance: air in must equal air out. Lab HVAC is exhaust-driven — fume hoods and lab exhaust are the boss, and the AHU exists to replace what they throw away.

Q16A lab should be held ___ to the corridor, and the corridor ___ to the labs.D
  • Apositive / negative
  • Bneutral / neutral
  • Cnegative / negative
  • Dnegative / positive

WhyAir flows clean → dirty. Exhaust exceeds supply in the lab so fumes stay in; the corridor runs supply-heavy so clean air pushes inward.

Q17Why is a building normally kept slightly POSITIVE to the outside?B
  • ABecause code sets a maximum pressure
  • BSo unconditioned outside air isn't sucked in through every crack — no drafts, no unfiltered humidity
  • CTo make the fans work harder
  • DTo keep the roof drains flowing

WhySupply is set a little higher than total exhaust, so the surplus leaks out. Go negative and outside air infiltrates: cold drafts in winter, sticky humidity in summer, all unfiltered.

Q18If you double the outside airflow (CFM) through the AHU, the energy needed to condition it roughly…A
  • ADoubles
  • BStays the same
  • CGoes up four times
  • DHalves

WhyConditioning energy is linear in airflow (1.08 × CFM × ΔT for sensible heat). That's why airflow — not setpoints — is the biggest energy lever, and why the ERM matters so much.

Q19When does the ERM do useful work?C
  • AOnly when the chiller is off
  • BSummer only, pre-cooling the incoming air
  • CBoth — it preheats incoming air in winter and pre-cools it in summer
  • DWinter only, preheating the incoming air

WhyThe glycol loop simply moves energy from the warmer airstream to the cooler one. In winter the exhaust is warmer than the OA (preheat); in summer the exhaust is cooler than the OA (pre-cool). Only in mild shoulder weather is there nothing to recover.

Q20What is a unit heater (UH) usually there to do?A
  • AHeat a space the ductwork doesn't reach — often for freeze protection of pipes
  • BBoost the exhaust fan
  • CHumidify the labs
  • DCool a server room

WhyDocks, vestibules, stairs, garages, mechanical rooms. Their real job is frequently keeping sprinkler and water lines above freezing — a failed UH in January is a burst-pipe call.

Q21The biggest difference between office air and lab air is…A
  • AOffice air is recirculated; lab air is 100% outside air, used once and exhausted
  • BLabs use more filters
  • COffice air skips the AHU
  • DOffice air is colder

WhyOffices reuse their air (cheap, comfort-driven). Labs cannot recirculate at all — conditioned once, used once, out the LEF. That single difference drives the cost, the pressure, and the controls.

Q22An economizer saves energy in an OFFICE AHU by…D
  • AShutting the exhaust fans
  • BRunning the chiller harder
  • CRecirculating lab air
  • DOpening the OA dampers wide in mild weather to cool with fresh air instead of the chiller

WhyFree cooling on the air side. A lab AHU has no use for it — it is already at 100% outside air, so the ERM is where its savings come from instead.

Q23How does an FPT differ from a plain VAV box?A
  • AIt has its own fan that pulls in return air and blends it with the primary supply air; four-pipe versions have both a hot and a chilled coil
  • BIt replaces the AHU
  • CIt only serves labs
  • DIt has no coil

WhyThe fan and the return-air path are the difference. A two-pipe FPT usually has hot water only; a four-pipe FPT has hot AND chilled coils, so the same box can heat or cool — handy on a perimeter zone that swings all day.

Q24A room is 6,000 ft³ and gets 800 CFM of supply air. What is its air change rate?A
  • A8 ACH
  • B12 ACH
  • C4 ACH
  • D80 ACH

WhyACH = CFM × 60 ÷ volume = 800 × 60 ÷ 6,000 = 8. Or the other way: CFM = ACH × volume ÷ 60.

Q25In a hood-heavy lab with every sash wide open, what is governing the supply airflow?B
  • AThe chilled water setpoint
  • BThe hood exhaust — supply must rise to match whatever the hoods pull
  • CThe ACH requirement
  • DThe thermostat

WhyA lab has two demands for air — the ACH dilution requirement and the hood exhaust — and the larger one wins. Closing sashes lets airflow fall back to the ACH floor, which is why sash management is an energy program.

Open questions — model answers

These are guides, not scripts. Credit any answer that gets the mechanism right, in the new hire’s own words.

O1A lab room is too warm. Walk the heat out of the building — name every loop and machine it passes through.

A strong answer coversCareful — there are two different paths, and a lab uses the first one. (1) The VAV box does not cool anything; it just meters in more 55°F supply air. That cold air absorbs the room's heat, warms up, and leaves through the exhaust valve — so in a 100% outside-air lab the room's heat physically leaves the building with the exhaust, out the LEF. The ERM's exhaust coil grabs some of it on the way. Meanwhile the chiller was never cooling room air at all: the AHU's chilled-water coil is cooling the incoming outside air. (2) A recirculating unit — a CRAC/CRAH, or a four-pipe FPT with a chilled-water coil — pulls room air across its own coil. Only there does the room's heat enter the chilled water, and then: CHW pump to the chiller, refrigerant lifts it into the condenser water, CW pump to the cooling tower, evaporation to the atmosphere.

O2It is 30°F outside. Explain what free cooling is doing right now and why the compressors can be off.

A strong answer coversThe tower alone can make condenser water colder than the chilled-water setpoint. The free-cooling pump routes that cold tower water through a plate heat exchanger against the chilled loop, so the CHW gets cooled directly — no refrigeration cycle, compressors off, and the plant runs on pump and fan energy only.

O3Explain to a tenant why their lab cannot recirculate air like an office does — and what the ERM does about the cost.

A strong answer coversLab air may carry fumes and hazards, so it is used once and exhausted; every cubic foot in is fresh outside air that must be fully conditioned. The ERM softens that cost: glycol run-around coils pull heat from the outgoing exhaust and hand it to the incoming air without the airstreams ever touching.

O4How is the WSHP setup different from the central plant — where does heating and cooling actually happen, and where does fresh air come from?

A strong answer coversInstead of one plant conditioning all the air, each zone has its own heat pump rejecting or absorbing heat on a shared water loop; the fluid cooler and boiler only trim the loop temperature. Temperature is handled at the zone; fresh air comes separately from an HV/MAU, and exhaust takes it back out.

O5The chilled-water pump trips. What symptoms show up, and in what order?

A strong answer coversNo CHW flow means the coils stop absorbing heat: supply air temperature climbs first, then space temperatures drift up, hot calls start, and the chiller may alarm or unload on low flow. The fix chain starts at the pump — no pump, no flow, no cooling.

O6A restroom smells in the corridor, and on the same cold morning the lobby doors are hard to open. Explain both with pressure — and say what you'd check.

A strong answer coversOdor migrating out means the restroom is no longer negative: its GEF is likely down or a damper shut, so exhaust no longer exceeds supply there. The hard lobby door is stack effect — warm air rising makes the lower floors negative to outside, so the door fights you. Check the GEF and its damper first, then look at overall balance (supply vs. total exhaust) to see whether the building itself has drifted negative on top of the stack effect.

O7A tenant asks why their lab costs so much more to run than the office suite next door, when both are in the same building on the same chillers. Explain it.

A strong answer coversThe plant is shared, but the air is not. The office recirculates most of its air and only conditions a small fraction of fresh outside air, so a mild day costs almost nothing — it can even economize and cool with fresh air. The lab runs 100% outside air: every cubic foot is heated or cooled from whatever the weather is, used once, and thrown away out the LEF, and its airflow is set by exhaust and hood safety rather than by the thermostat. The ERM buys some of that energy back, but a lab typically runs three to five times the energy per square foot.

Q1What is the ‘brain’ that everything in life safety reports to?D
  • AThe sprinkler riser
  • BThe BMS
  • CThe generator
  • DThe FACP — fire alarm control panel

WhyEvery detector and pull station reports to the FACP, and the FACP commands everything else.

Q2On an alarm, the FACP does which three things?B
  • AStarts the chiller, closes valves, silences horns
  • BNotifies occupants, signals the fire department, commands the zone controls
  • CCalls the elevator, opens windows, starts the AHU
  • DOnly sounds the horns

WhyAlert people, bring the fire department, and command the building — smoke control, doors, elevators.

Q3Sprinkler heads open…A
  • AOne at a time, each triggered by heat right over the fire
  • BWhen the pull station is pulled
  • COnly when the FACP commands them
  • DAll at once like the movies

WhyEach head is its own heat-activated valve. Movies lie — one head over the fire opens, the rest stay shut.

Q4The fire pump’s job is to…C
  • ADrain the system
  • BFill the tower
  • CBoost water pressure so sprinklers and standpipes get real flow
  • DCirculate hot water

WhyCity pressure alone often is not enough, especially up high — the fire pump provides the boost.

Q5What does the jockey pump do?C
  • APumps the neutralization tank
  • BBacks up the fire pump if it fails
  • CHolds system pressure day-to-day so the fire pump only starts on real flow
  • DTests the sprinklers weekly

WhyThe little jockey tops up small pressure losses. If pressure drops fast — real flow — the big pump starts, and that start is itself an alarm signal.

Q6A floor control valve assembly includes…B
  • AA smoke detector and horn
  • BValve, tamper switch, flow switch, test & drain
  • CA PRV and an RPZ
  • DJust a shutoff valve

WhyPer floor: an isolation valve (with a tamper switch watching it), a flow switch to detect discharge, and a test & drain to prove it all works.

Q7‘Zone control’ in life safety refers to…D
  • AThe sprinkler floor valves
  • BThermostat zones
  • CThe security card readers
  • DSmoke/fire dampers, door release, stair pressurization, elevator recall — commanded by the FACP

WhyWhen a zone alarms, the FACP closes its dampers, releases its doors, pressurizes stairs, and recalls elevators.

Q8Stair pressurization exists to…C
  • AVentilate the roof
  • BCool the stairwells
  • CKeep smoke out of the egress stairs so people can escape
  • DDry the sprinkler pipes

WhyFans blow air into the stairs so pressure pushes smoke out — the escape path stays clear.

Q9Pre-action sprinkler piping is used where…A
  • AAn accidental discharge would be unacceptable — it needs detection AND a fused head before water flows
  • BThere are no detectors
  • CFreezing is impossible
  • DWater is cheap

WhyTwo independent triggers (detection fills the pipe, heat opens the head) protect spaces where a false discharge would be a disaster.

Q10On a utility power loss, the correct sequence is…B
  • AEverything transfers instantly with no delay
  • BATS senses loss → start signal → generator builds the emergency bus → ATSs transfer, life safety first
  • CGenerator builds bus → ATS senses → transfer
  • DFire pump starts → generator follows

WhySense → start → build the bus → transfer. Life-safety loads must be live within seconds; standby follows.

Q11Why does the generator get a weekly test?D
  • ATo charge its battery only
  • BCode requires daily fuel checks
  • CTo warm the building
  • DBecause a generator that has not run is a generator you cannot trust — the test proves the whole start-and-transfer chain

WhyThe weekly run proves it starts, takes load, and the ATSs will have real power to transfer to.

Q12Duct detectors exist to…A
  • ADetect smoke in the airstream and shut down the AHU so it is not spread through the building
  • BSense humidity
  • CCount filter changes
  • DMeasure airflow

WhyIf smoke gets into the ductwork, the duct detector stops the air handler from distributing it.

Q13‘Lab exhaust fails safe’ means…A
  • AExhaust keeps running — it is on emergency power, because fume control must never stop
  • BIt only runs during business hours
  • CIt shuts off in a fire
  • DIt reverses direction

WhyFume hoods and lab exhaust are life safety for the people at the bench — they ride the generator and never stop.

Open questions — model answers

These are guides, not scripts. Credit any answer that gets the mechanism right, in the new hire’s own words.

O1A smoke detector activates on the third floor. Walk the first sixty seconds — name everything the FACP commands.

A strong answer coversThe detector reports to the FACP. It sounds horns and strobes, signals the fire department through monitoring, and commands the zone: smoke/fire dampers close, held-open doors release, stair pressurization fans start, elevators recall to the safe floor, and the AHU shuts down or flips to smoke mode.

O2Explain wet, dry, and pre-action piping — and give a space where each one belongs.

A strong answer coversWet pipe is water-filled and simplest — most occupied spaces. Dry pipe holds air and fills only on discharge — spaces that can freeze, like a parking garage or loading dock. Pre-action needs detection AND a fused head before water flows — spaces where an accidental discharge is unacceptable, like an electrical room or server room.

O3One sprinkler head opens over a fire. Why does the fire pump start — and what was the jockey pump doing before this?

A strong answer coversThe jockey had been holding system pressure, topping up small losses. A head opening dumps pressure faster than the jockey can keep up, so the fire pump controller sees the drop and starts the big pump — and that pressure drop and flow are themselves alarm signals to the FACP.

O4Explain zone control to a tenant: what physically happens on their floor during an alarm?

A strong answer coversTheir floor is a zone. On alarm, its smoke dampers close to hold the smoke in that compartment, magnetically-held doors release and swing shut, the stair pressurization fans keep their escape stairs clear, and the elevators stop serving the floor and recall — all commanded automatically by the fire alarm panel.

O5Utility power just dropped. Describe the whole generator/ATS sequence until the building is back to normal.

A strong answer coversEach ATS senses the loss and sends the start signal; the generator comes up to speed and voltage and builds the emergency bus in about ten seconds; the ATSs transfer their loads onto it, life safety first, standby after. When the utility returns and holds, the ATSs retransfer on a delay, and the generator runs unloaded to cool down, then stops.

Plumbing

Multiple choice — with explanations

Q1RPZ stands for…D
  • AReverse Pipe Zone
  • BRapid Pressure Zone
  • CRegulated Potable Zone
  • DReduced Pressure Zone

WhyReduced Pressure Zone: two checks in series with a relief valve keeping the middle zone at lower pressure — the highest level of backflow protection.

Q2What is an RPZ actually protecting?B
  • AThe building’s fixtures
  • BThe city’s drinking water, from anything flowing backward out of the building
  • CThe water heater
  • DThe booster pump

WhyBackflow protection is about everyone outside the building — nothing in here may ever siphon back into the public main.

Q3The rule for potable vs non-potable piping is…B
  • APotable is always bigger pipe
  • BNever cross-connected — non-potable runs on its own marked, labeled piping
  • CNon-potable is always underground
  • DThey can share pipe if labeled

WhyTwo systems, never connected. The non-potable side is kept separate, marked, and labeled.

Q4The moment potable water crosses an RPZ into a mechanical system, it…A
  • ABecomes non-potable
  • BBecomes distilled
  • CMust be heated
  • DStays potable

WhyOnce it is on the far side feeding a loop, a tower, or an RO/DI skid, it is non-potable from that point on — and the RPZ keeps it from ever coming back.

Q5Cooling tower make-up water can be fed by…D
  • ASanitary grey water untreated
  • BStorm water directly
  • CThe chilled water loop
  • DReclaimed / non-potable where available, or potable through a PRV + RPZ

WhyEither grade works — what is non-negotiable is the backflow protection on any potable feed.

Q6Why store domestic hot water at ~140°F but deliver ~120°F?B
  • ATo save energy
  • BHot storage controls Legionella; the mixing valve tempers it down to a safe delivery temperature
  • CCode bans hot water above 120°F everywhere
  • DPipes cannot handle 140°F

WhyStore and circulate hot enough to keep Legionella from growing, temper at the mixing valve so no one gets scalded.

Q7The recirc loop exists so that…C
  • AThe heater lasts longer
  • BCold water arrives faster
  • CHot water is hot at every tap and never sits in warm dead legs where Legionella grows
  • DPressure stays constant

WhyMoving water stays hot and safe; still, lukewarm water in dead legs is the Legionella risk.

Q8The vent stack’s job is to…A
  • ALet air in and sewer gas out so drains flow and traps keep their seal
  • BCatch rainwater
  • CCool the sanitary line
  • DExhaust lab fumes

WhyWithout vents, drains gurgle and traps siphon dry — then sewer gas walks right in.

Q9A P-trap works by…A
  • AHolding a water seal that blocks sewer gas from coming up the drain
  • BA rubber flap
  • CA spring-loaded valve
  • DAir pressure

WhyThat U of standing water is the whole trick — which is why a dry trap in an unused floor drain smells.

Q10Corrosive lab waste reaches the sewer by…C
  • AThe storm leader
  • BRegular PVC drain straight out
  • CAcid-resistant piping to a neutralization tank, pH corrected first, then sanitary
  • DEvaporation

WhyNeutralize before the sewer — the tank brings pH into the safe range before discharge.

Q11The RO/DI system is fed by…D
  • AThe hot water loop
  • BCondensate
  • CNon-potable reclaimed water
  • DPotable water through its own RPZ, producing lab process water on dedicated piping

WhyPotable in through an RPZ; ultra-pure process water out on its own piping to the labs.

Q12Roof drains discharge to…C
  • AThe cooling tower
  • BSanitary, like everything else
  • CThe storm system — completely separate piping from sanitary
  • DThe neutralization tank

WhyRain and only rain goes to storm — a separate system that typically discharges untreated to waterways.

Q13A loading-dock drain routes…B
  • ATo the roof
  • BThrough an oil/water separator, then to sanitary — never storm
  • CStraight to storm since it is outdoors
  • DInto the RO/DI feed

WhyFuel and oil drips make it ‘touched something’ — separator traps the oil, only the water continues to sanitary.

Q14Cooling tower blowdown goes to…A
  • ASanitary — it carries treatment chemicals and concentrated solids
  • BStorm — it is just water
  • CBack into the make-up line
  • DThe irrigation system

WhyBiocides and concentrated minerals mean sanitary, often through an air gap and under a discharge permit. Never storm.

Open questions — model answers

These are guides, not scripts. Credit any answer that gets the mechanism right, in the new hire’s own words.

O1Trace a drop of city water to a lab faucet — name every device it passes and what each one does.

A strong answer coversThrough the meter (measurement and the main shutoff), the RPZ backflow preventer (protects the city supply), the PRV (drops street pressure to a safe level), and the booster pump (pressure for the upper floors) — then out through the cold distribution to the fixture; the hot side adds the water heater, recirc loop, and mixing valve.

O2Explain the potable / non-potable rule, and what crossing the RPZ means — use tower make-up as your example.

A strong answer coversPotable serves people, non-potable serves machines, and the two are never cross-connected. Tower make-up can come from reclaimed water, or from potable — but the moment potable passes through the PRV + RPZ toward the tower, it is non-potable from that point on, and the RPZ guarantees it can never flow back.

O3Why store hot water at about 140°F, deliver it at about 120°F, and keep it moving? Name the risk each part manages.

A strong answer coversStoring and circulating at 140°F keeps Legionella from growing; the recirc loop prevents warm, stagnant dead legs where it breeds; and the mixing valve tempers delivery to about 120°F so nobody is scalded. Hot enough to be safe from bacteria, cool enough to be safe on skin.

O4Four drains: a restroom sink, a lab sink, a loading-dock trench drain, and a roof drain. Where does each go, and why?

A strong answer coversRestroom sink: trap, then sanitary. Lab sink: acid-resistant pipe to the neutralization tank, pH corrected, then sanitary. Dock drain: through the oil/water separator, then sanitary — oil never goes to either sewer directly. Roof drain: rain and only rain, so it runs on the storm system, completely separate from sanitary.

O5You find an RPZ discharging water from its relief port. What is it telling you, and what do you check?

A strong answer coversThe relief valve dumps whenever the middle zone cannot stay below supply pressure — so either a check valve is fouled or a real backflow condition occurred. It is doing its job, not failing. Check the checks for debris, verify pressures, and get it tested — never plug the relief port.

  • CFeeder → main → branch
  • DBranch → feeder → main

Q4You find a tripped branch breaker. The rule is…D

  • BReset it repeatedly until it holds
  • CReplace it immediately
  • DInvestigate, reset once, and never hold one in against a fault

WhyA trip is information. One reset after looking for a cause; if it trips again, something is wrong — escalate, don’t fight it.

Q5An ATS has…D
  • ANo moving parts
  • BOne input and two outputs
  • CThree inputs
  • DTwo inputs — normal and emergency — and one output

Q6On utility loss, the correct sequence is…D
  • AUPS starts the generator
  • BGenerator builds bus, then ATS notices
  • CEverything shuts down safely
  • DATS senses loss → sends start signal → generator builds the emergency bus → ATSs transfer, life safety first

WhySense → start signal → bus builds in ~10 seconds → transfer. Life safety must be live within seconds; standby follows.

Open questions — model answers

These are guides, not scripts. Credit any answer that gets the mechanism right, in the new hire’s own words.

O1You find the generator running. List, in order, what you read before touching anything — and name every reason the generator might legitimately be running.

A strong answer coversRead five things, in order: (1) the ATS (Automatic Transfer Switch) position, Normal or Emergency — read at the switch itself: the controller screen, the Source Available and Load On lamps, the Not-In-Auto lamp, the mechanical position indicator, and on a bypass-isolation unit the bypass and isolation handle positions, all from the front without opening the door; if Emergency, that generator is the building's power. (2) The utility: volts on all three legs, or not. (3) Who started it — and was it you? The person who starts a generator is the person who stops it. If you did not start it, you do not stop it; go find whoever did. (4) The controller mode: AUTO, exercise running, cool-down timer counting, or not in AUTO. (5) The schedule and the work orders. Legitimate reasons it runs: a utility outage; a utility anomaly such as a brownout, single-phasing or a frequency excursion; the weekly no-load exercise; a monthly loaded test; an annual load bank test; a cool-down after retransfer; a manual start by a technician; the ATS test switch simulating an outage; peak shaving or demand response if the site is enrolled. The tenth possibility is a sensing fault — a drifted voltage relay, a loose neutral, a failed phase monitor — where the utility is fine but the ATS believes otherwise, and that one you investigate. In six of those ten the building is completely normal, which is why looking at the building tells you nothing. You stop a running generator for exactly one reason: a genuine emergency in front of you.

O2Trace power from the street to a lab receptacle — name every transformation and breaker on the way.

A strong answer coversUtility medium voltage into the transformer, stepped to 480V; through the main breaker and a feeder breaker in the switchgear; to a distribution panel, through a dry-type step-down transformer making 208/120V; then a branch breaker in the receptacle panel feeds the outlet.

O3The building goes dark for ten seconds and comes back. Explain what happened — and why the −80 freezers and the BMS never noticed.

A strong answer coversUtility dropped; the ATSs sensed it and signaled the generator, which built the emergency bus in about ten seconds, and the ATSs transferred — that was the gap. The freezers rode it out on generator-backed red-outlet circuits, and the BMS never blinked at all because it sits behind the UPS, whose batteries covered the seconds the generator needed.

O4Explain the breaker hierarchy and the reset-once rule — why not just keep resetting a breaker that trips?

A strong answer coversMain at the service, feeders in the switchgear, branch breakers in the panels — each protects the wiring downstream of it. A trip means overload or fault; you investigate, reset once, and if it trips again something is genuinely wrong. Repeated resetting or holding a breaker in feeds energy into a fault — that is how equipment burns and arc flash happens.

O5Sort these onto normal, generator/standby, and UPS power, with reasoning: hallway lighting, lab exhaust fans, a −80 freezer, the BMS servers, general receptacles.

A strong answer coversHallway lighting and general receptacles: normal (egress lighting specifically is on emergency). Lab exhaust and the −80 freezer: generator/standby — they must survive an outage but can ride the ten-second gap. The BMS servers: UPS — they cannot tolerate even a blink, then the generator carries them long-term.

O6You need to open a panel to work on a circuit. Walk the safety steps before you touch anything.

A strong answer coversRead the arc-flash label for the hazard level and required PPE. Identify and open the upstream disconnect, apply your lock and tag (LOTO), then verify dead with a tester — live-dead-live. Only trained and authorized people work in energized gear; until it is proven dead, treat every conductor as live.

Multiple choice — with explanations

Q1A BMS controller does not make heat or cold. What does it actually do?B
  • AIt generates chilled water
  • BIt decides when equipment runs and how far valves and dampers open
  • CIt replaces the electrician
  • DIt powers the fans

WhyThe plant makes heat and cold; the BMS decides when equipment runs and how far each valve, damper, and drive is driven. A BMS in alarm is telling you about equipment, not about itself.

Q2What are the three parts of every control loop?C
  • APanel, breaker, motor
  • BChiller, pump, tower
  • CSensor, controller, actuator
  • DAlarm, trend, schedule

WhyA sensor measures, a controller compares the measurement to setpoint, an actuator changes something. Then the sensor measures again. That circle is the loop.

Q3The measured value the controller reads is called the…D
  • ADeadband
  • BOutput
  • CSetpoint
  • DProcess variable

WhySetpoint is what you want. Process variable is what you have. The difference between them is the error, and the controller works to drive the error to zero.

Q4A valve is hunting — swinging open and closed and never settling. Most likely cause?A
  • ALoop tuning: too much gain, or integral wound up
  • BWrong voltage at the actuator
  • CThe sensor is unplugged
  • DThe schedule is off

WhyHunting is a tuning symptom. Excess proportional gain overreacts to small errors; too much integral keeps pushing after the error is gone. It stresses the actuator and wears the valve.

Q5What is a deadband, and why does it exist?B
  • AThe range a sensor cannot read
  • BA range around setpoint where nothing is commanded — it stops equipment short-cycling
  • CThe time a controller stays offline
  • DA network fault

WhyWithout a deadband the equipment would chase every fraction of a degree. The deadband is what stops the heating and cooling from fighting each other around the same setpoint.

Q6A point reads Commanded: ON — Status: OFF. What have you learned?C
  • ANothing — they always differ
  • BThe sensor is broken
  • CThe BMS asked, and the equipment did not answer. Investigate the equipment, not the graphic
  • DThe setpoint is wrong

WhyCommand is what the BMS asked for. Status is proof it happened, usually from a current switch or an auxiliary contact. Command without status is a real fault, and it is the single most useful pair of points on any graphic.

Q7A temperature point has been reading exactly 55.0°F for three days. What is that?A
  • AA stale point — communication is lost or the sensor is failed. Never trust a flat line
  • BA deadband
  • CPerfect control
  • DA schedule change

WhyReal sensors wiggle. A perfectly flat value is a sensor that stopped reporting or a controller that stopped talking. The trend is what exposes it.

Q8What does an interlock or permissive do?C
  • ALocks the front-end screen
  • BSends the alarm email
  • CPrevents equipment from starting until safe conditions are proven
  • DTies two setpoints together

WhyThe chiller does not start until flow is proven; the fan does not start with a closed damper. An interlock is a condition, not a suggestion — never defeat one to make an alarm go away.

Q9Where does the fire alarm sit relative to the BMS?C
  • AThe BMS controls the fire alarm
  • BThe BMS silences alarms
  • CThe FACP is a stand-alone system. The BMS may monitor it, but it does not control it
  • DThey are the same panel

WhyLife safety is stand-alone by code. The FACP commands dampers, fans, and elevators directly. The BMS may watch, and hardwired interlocks always beat a software command.

Q10Why does the BMS sit behind a UPS?D
  • ASo it can run the elevators
  • BTo save energy
  • CTo reduce alarms
  • DSo it never sees the ten-second gap before the generator picks up

WhyIf the BMS rebooted during every transfer, the building would lose its controls exactly when it most needs them. The BMS rides through on the UPS.

Q11An operator leaves a point in Hand / Override. What is the consequence?B
  • AThe point is deleted
  • BThe BMS no longer controls it — schedules, resets, and alarms are all bypassed
  • CIt improves control
  • DNothing, it reverts at midnight

WhyAn override is a promise to come back. Overrides left in place are the single most common cause of 'the BMS is broken' calls — and of unexplained energy use.

Q12What is a reset schedule (for example, outdoor-air reset)?C
  • ARebooting a controller on a timer
  • BA calendar of holidays
  • CA setpoint that slides automatically with a measured condition
  • DClearing the alarm log

WhyHot-water supply temperature rises as it gets colder outside; static pressure or DP setpoints fall when valves are mostly closed. Reset schedules are among the biggest energy levers a BMS has.

Q13BACnet MS/TP and BACnet/IP are…B
  • AAlarm classes
  • BCommunication protocols — a shared language on the field bus and over the network
  • CSensor types
  • DTwo brands of controller

WhyProtocols let controllers from different manufacturers talk. MS/TP runs on a twisted-pair field bus; BACnet/IP runs over the building network. Modbus and LON are others you will meet.

Q14The four basic point types on a controller are…A
  • AAI, AO, BI, BO — analog and binary, in and out
  • BAlarm, trend, schedule, graphic
  • CSensor, valve, damper, fan
  • DP, I, D, and E

WhyAnalog Input reads a varying signal (a temperature). Analog Output drives a varying signal (a valve). Binary Input reads on/off (a status contact). Binary Output commands on/off (a start relay).

Q15Before you change a setpoint on the graphic, what should you do first?B
  • APut the point in Hand
  • BLook at the trend — understand what the system has actually been doing
  • CReboot the controller
  • DSilence the alarm

WhyTrend before you touch. A setpoint change made blind fixes a symptom on one screen and creates a problem on another. The trend is the only place the system tells you its history.

Q16A damper actuator is spring return, normally closed. On loss of power it will…B
  • AAlarm and hold
  • BDrive closed
  • CDrive open
  • DStay where it was

WhyFail position is a design decision, not an accident. Outside-air dampers usually fail closed to protect coils from freezing; some safety dampers fail open. Know which is which before winter.

Q17What is cascade control, in plain terms?C
  • ATwo alarms in a row
  • BA backup controller
  • COne loop's output becomes another loop's setpoint
  • DA network topology

WhyA room-temperature loop sets the supply-air temperature setpoint, and a second loop drives the valve to hit that. The slow loop steers the fast one.

Q18A nuisance alarm floods the log every few minutes. The right response is to…A
  • AFind the cause — a bad sensor, a too-tight limit, or a real fault — not to disable the alarm
  • BWiden every limit
  • CIgnore the log
  • DDelete the alarm

WhyAlarm fatigue is a safety issue: when everything alarms, nothing does. Fix the cause or retune the limit deliberately, and record why.

Q19A modulating chilled-water valve has sat at 100% open for four days. No alarm has fired. What is going on?C
  • APerfect control
  • BThe valve is in Hand
  • CThe loop has saturated — it is asking for everything it has and still missing setpoint
  • DNothing, that is normal for summer

WhyA device built to modulate is supposed to move. Pinned at 100% (or 0%) means the load exceeds capacity, a sensor is lying, or the setpoint is unachievable. It rarely alarms, because nothing crossed a limit — the controller is simply trying its hardest. You find out on the first hot day when there is no reserve left.

Q20A tenant calls about a warm room. What do you do first?B
  • AChange the space setpoint
  • BOpen the BMS: schedule, alarms, trends, command vs status, any overrides
  • CReset the controller
  • DWalk to the AHU

WhyEvery troubleshooting call and operational check starts at the BMS. You should arrive at the equipment with a hypothesis, not a flashlight. The exception is smoke, water, or anything life-safety — go now, and read the trend afterwards.

Q21A status point says a fan is running. What has that actually proven?D
  • AThat airflow is at design
  • BThat the fan shaft is turning
  • CThat the damper is open
  • DOnly as much as the device behind it — a current switch may prove the VFD is energized, not that the shaft turns

WhyThree layers can disagree: what was commanded, what the status point claims, and what the metal is doing. An aux contact can prove the starter pulled in, not that the coupling is intact. Verify at the hardware before you override anything.

Q22You need to know why a room was cold last Tuesday, but that point was never being logged. What can you do?A
  • ANothing — you cannot trend the past. Set the trend up now, then wait
  • BRecover it from the alarm log
  • CReboot the controller to rebuild history
  • DIncrease the sample interval

WhyIf a point was never logged, that history does not exist and never will. When you meet a problem you cannot explain, the first action is often to set up the trend, then let the building tell you.

Q23Which four points should you overlay on almost any trend?D
  • AVoltage, current, frequency, power
  • BFour days in a row
  • CFour different rooms
  • DProcess variable, setpoint, output %, and command/status

WhyThe PV shows what happened; the setpoint shows what was asked; the output % shows how hard the loop worked, and reveals saturation; command and status show whether the equipment was even running. Never trend a reading without its setpoint.

Q24Point classification — space temperature on an AHU graphic is:B
  • AAV — a setpoint
  • BAI — a reading
  • CBI — a status
  • DAO — a command

WhyIt comes from the world and it varies, so it is an analog input. It is a reading — and it means nothing until you put its setpoint beside it.

Q25Point classification — the chilled-water supply temperature setpoint is:A
  • ANone of AI/AO/BI/BO — it is a stored value (BACnet AV), and its role is setpoint
  • BBI — a status
  • CAO — a command
  • DAI — a reading

WhyThe trap. Nothing senses a setpoint. It is a value living in the controller, written by a person or by a reset schedule. Inputs come from the world, outputs go to the world — setpoints come from us.

Q26Point classification — the supply fan start/stop issued by the BMS is:D
  • AAO — a command
  • BBI — a status
  • CAI — a reading
  • DBO — a command

WhyOn or off, going out to the world: a binary output. It is what the BMS asked for — which is not the same as proof it happened.

Q27Point classification — the supply fan current switch is:C
  • ABO — a command
  • BAI — a reading
  • CBI — a status
  • DAV — a setpoint

WhyOn or off, coming in from the world: a binary input. Its role is status — independent proof the motor is drawing current. Command ON with this reading OFF is a fault.

Q28Point classification — a cooling valve driven to 45% is:A
  • AAO — a command
  • BBO — a command
  • CAI — a status
  • DAI — a reading

WhyA varying signal going out: analog output. If the graphic also shows position feedback from the actuator, that feedback is an AI acting as a status — a different point entirely.

Q29Point classification — a damper end switch is:B
  • AAO — a command
  • BBI — a status
  • CBO — a command
  • DAI — a reading

WhyOn or off, coming in: binary input, and its job is to prove the blade actually reached the end of travel. An actuator can stroke while its linkage is loose.

Q30Point classification — a VFD speed command from the controller is:D
  • ABO — a command
  • BAV — a setpoint
  • CAI — a reading
  • DAO — a command

WhyA varying signal going out: analog output. The speed the VFD reports back is a separate AI, and that one is a status.

Q31Point classification — filter differential pressure is:A
  • AAI — a reading
  • BAV — a setpoint
  • CAO — a command
  • DBI — a status

WhyA varying signal coming in: analog input, and it is a reading. Compare it to its alarm limit — the number alone tells you nothing.

Q32Is the process variable an input or a status?A
  • AUsually an input, and never a status — a status pairs with a command, a PV pairs with a setpoint
  • BAlways a binary input
  • CAlways a status
  • DBoth mean the same thing

WhyA status is independent proof that a command was carried out, almost always binary. A process variable is the reading a loop controls to. PV is a role, not a point type: it can be calculated, arrive over the network, or even be binary.

Q33In BACnet, which objects have a Present_Value property?D

  • BOnly inputs, never outputs
  • COnly the ones being trended
  • DEvery object — AI, AO, BI, BO and AV alike. An AO's Present_Value is the command it is sending

  • BThe fan current switch
  • CThe fan start/stop relay
  • DThe chilled-water supply temperature setpoint

WhyA setpoint lives only in the controller's memory — no terminal, no wire, no I/O slot. The other three are hardware points landed on terminals with real devices behind them. When a hardware point is wrong you go to the field; when a software point is wrong you go to the logic.

O1Name every BMS vendor in use on this site, and list which buildings each vendor covers.

A strong answer coversThere is no generic answer — it is different in every portfolio, and that is exactly why it is the first question. Fill it in at the front end, from the site's controls documentation and from your mentor, and do not accept a verbal answer you cannot verify on a screen. For each vendor record: the product name, where the front end lives and how you log in, every building on it, the protocol carrying the traffic, and who to call when it breaks. A campus is rarely one system — older buildings carry an older front end, newer construction arrives with whatever was specified that year, and the two are stitched together over BACnet. Know which product you are logged into before you trust a screen, because the graphics, point naming, trend tools, and override procedure all differ.

O2A room is too warm. Walk from the complaint to the fix using the BMS, naming what you look at in order.

A strong answer coversStart with the trend, not the setpoint. Check the space temperature against its setpoint, and whether the zone is in occupied mode on the schedule. Look at the terminal unit: is the damper commanded open, and does the airflow reading confirm it? Check whether the reheat valve is commanded shut but the discharge temperature says otherwise — a leaking valve. Then look upstream: supply-air temperature at the AHU against its setpoint, static pressure or DP against its setpoint, and whether any point in the chain is in Hand. Only then consider changing anything, and change one thing at a time.

O3Explain the difference between a commanded value, a status point, and a setpoint. Why does an operator need all three?

A strong answer coversThe setpoint is the target you want. The commanded value is what the BMS is asking the equipment to do. The status is independent proof of what actually happened — a current switch, an auxiliary contact, a proven flow. Setpoint tells you intent, command tells you the controller's decision, status tells you reality. A fault hides in the gap between command and status, and a comfort complaint usually hides in the gap between setpoint and process variable.

O4Why is a flat trend line suspicious, and what would you do about it?

A strong answer coversA real sensor always shows small movement — noise, load changes, control action. A perfectly flat line means the value is no longer being updated: comm loss to the controller, a failed sensor, or a point stuck in override. Check the point's last-updated time, look at neighbouring points on the same controller to see whether the whole device went quiet, verify at the field device if you can, and never make a control decision on a stale value.

O5The BMS shows an AHU running normally, but the space is not being ventilated. Give at least three ways the graphic can be right and the building still wrong.

A strong answer coversThe graphic shows command, not reality: a fan commanded on with no status proof. A point left in Hand shows the value someone typed, not what the sensor reads. A sensor can be drifted or failed and reporting a plausible number. A damper actuator can be disconnected from its blade — the actuator strokes, the blade does not move. A filter can be loaded so the fan runs but the airflow is a fraction of design. And a point can be stale from comm loss while still displaying its last good value.

O6Why is the fire alarm system separate from the BMS, and what does that mean when you are troubleshooting?

A strong answer coversLife safety is required to stand alone so that a failure, a reboot, or a bad graphic in the BMS cannot compromise it. The FACP commands smoke dampers, fans, elevator recall, and door releases through hardwired paths. The BMS may monitor those points, but a hardwired interlock always overrides a software command. In troubleshooting, this means the BMS can be telling the truth about what it commanded while the FACP has overridden the equipment — and it means you never try to fix a life-safety device from a BMS graphic.

O7Why is a device pinned at 100% output more dangerous than a device in alarm?

A strong answer coversBecause nothing crossed a limit, so nothing alarms. From the controller's point of view it is behaving correctly — it is asking for full output and still not reaching setpoint. Meanwhile the loop has no reserve left, energy is being spent at maximum, and the underlying cause is hidden: either the load genuinely exceeds capacity, a sensor is lying so the loop chases a target it can never hit, or the setpoint is unachievable as configured. An alarm at least announces itself. A saturated output is silent, and you discover it on the first hot day when there is nothing left to give.
