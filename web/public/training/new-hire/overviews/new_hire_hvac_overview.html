How HVAC Works in a Life-Science BuildingThe base-building plant, in plain terms

A life-science building has to do something a normal office doesn't: bring in huge amounts of fresh outside air and throw it right back out, because lab air can't be recirculated. The base building is the central plant that makes this possible — it makes cold water, makes hot water, and moves big volumes of air in and out. Everything the plant does ends up at the AHU, which conditions the air and sends it to the spaces. We run that plant; tenants fine-tune the last few degrees in their own rooms.

01

Start here — what is HVAC?

Before any of the equipment names, the whole trade fits in one sentence: HVAC keeps the air in a building at the right temperature, the right humidity, clean, and always moving.

H — Heating

warm it up

Making the air warmer when it’s cold. In this building, a boiler heats water, and that hot water heats the air.

V — Ventilation

keep it fresh

Bringing in fresh outside air and pushing stale air out. This is the big one in a lab — the air only goes through once.

AC — Air Conditioning

cool it & dry it

Making the air cooler and less humid. A chiller makes cold water, and that cold water cools the air.

💡 The one idea that makes everything else make sense Only two things travel around this building: air and water.

Air is what people actually breathe. It flows through ducts to every room.
Water never touches the air. It runs in pipes and its only job is to carry heat from one place to another — chilled water absorbs heat from the air blowing across its coil and carries it away; hot water releases its heat into the air the same way.

Everything you’ll learn is either a machine that moves air (fans, AHU), a machine that moves water (pumps), or a machine that changes the temperature of one using the other (a coil — where air blows across a pipe full of water).
❄ “Cooling” doesn’t make cold — it moves heat There is no such thing as making cold. Heat always flows from hotter to colder, and cooling just means taking heat out of a place and dumping it somewhere else.

A room gets cool because cold supply air absorbs its heat. That heat then leaves one of two ways: it rides the exhaust air straight out of the building (what happens in a lab), or it is handed to chilled water at a coil → carried to the chiller → pushed out to the cooling tower → released to the sky. Either way, the building’s heat doesn’t vanish. It leaves. Once that clicks, the whole plant is just a relay race for heat.

02

How it fits together

Three things flow through the building. The water sides are closed loops — pumps push the same water around and around, out to the coils and back — while the air passes through once (100% outside air, never recirculated). The AHU sits in all three, because it's where the water side and the air side meet.

The short version: two water loops and one air path. The condenser-water pump circulates water between the tower and the chiller (or, when it's cold out, a heat exchanger skips the chiller). The chilled-water pump pushes chilled water out to the AHU and CRAC coils — where it picks up heat — and back to the chiller to be re-cooled. The hot-water pump does the same on the heating side: boiler → coils → back to the boiler. The pumps are what make the loops go; no pump, no flow, no cooling or heat. Meanwhile the AHU pulls in fresh outside air, the ERM pre-conditions it (preheating it in winter, pre-cooling it in summer), the AHU sets its temperature and blows it to the terminal units in each space — and the LEF pulls the used lab air back out and high above the roof. Separately, general exhaust fans (GEFs) pull air straight out of the support spaces — restrooms, electrical and MDF rooms, elevator machine rooms, mechanical spaces — and dump it at the roof. That air never goes through the ERM or the LEF.

❄ Free cooling — the energy win When it's cold outside, the cooling tower can make condenser water cold enough to chill the building through a heat exchanger, so the chiller's compressors can rest. Same cold water, a fraction of the energy. That's why you'll see a separate free-cooling (condenser-water) pump and a free-cooling HX alongside the chiller — the plant switches to this mode automatically when the weather allows.

Heating water setpoints — three kinds of plant

There is no single “correct” hot-water temperature — it depends on what makes the heat. The return temperature matters as much as the supply.

Condensing boiler

high efficiency · 90–98%

Supply ~120–140°F, and the return must stay below ~130°F — that’s what lets flue gas condense and release its latent heat. Runs cooler on purpose; with outdoor reset it may sit near 120°F in mild weather.

Non-condensing boiler

conventional · 80–85%

Supply ~160–180°F, and the return must stay above ~140°F — the opposite rule. Cold return condenses flue gas inside a boiler not built for it, corroding the heat exchanger and thermally shocking it.

Steam-to-water HX

converter · campus/district steam

No boiler here — campus or district steam heats the loop through a heat exchanger (converter). A steam control valve modulates to hold supply ~140–180°F; the trap and condensate return are what you watch.

Outdoor reset slides the supply setpoint with the weather in any of the three — 180°F on the coldest day, far lower in mild weather. So a loop reading 130°F in April is not necessarily a problem; know which plant you have before you judge the number.

A few more ideas that explain a lot

Air in = air out

pressure

Whatever the fans blow in has to leave. We push in a little more than we pull out, so the building stays slightly positive and the extra leaks out through doors and cracks.

Why bother? If the building went negative, outside air would get sucked in through every crack — cold drafts in winter, sticky humidity in summer, all of it unfiltered.

Clean air flows to dirty

direction

Inside, each room is set up so air moves the safe way: a lab pulls out more than it gets, so it’s negative and fumes stay in. A corridor gets more than it loses, so it’s positive and pushes clean air toward the labs and restrooms — never the other way.

Every CFM costs money

energy

All that fresh air has to be heated or cooled from whatever the weather is. Double the airflow, double the energy. That’s why the ERM exists — and why we don’t move more air than we need.

Air changes per hour (ACH)

how much fresh air

How many times an hour a room’s entire volume of air is replaced. At 6 ACH, all the air in the room is swapped out six times every hour — once every ten minutes.

An office runs about 4–6 ACH. A lab typically runs 6–12, because the point there isn’t comfort — it’s diluting and removing whatever is in the air.

That’s the plain version. If you want the numbers, the offsets, and the field symptoms behind them, they’re waiting in Section 09 — Going deeper at the end of this page.

03

The nine pieces of the base-building plant

Cooling

Chiller

Makes the building's cold.

Uses a refrigeration cycle to chill water down to about 44°F and pumps it out to the AHU cooling coils.

Why it mattersNo chiller, no cooling — anywhere in the building.

Heating

Boiler

Makes the building's heat.

Burns gas to heat water — ~120–140°F if condensing, ~160–180°F if not — pumped to the AHU heating coils and reheat coils. Some buildings have no boiler at all and make hot water from campus steam through a heat exchanger.

Why it mattersHeat in winter, and the humidity labs need.

Cooling

Cooling Tower

Throws the building's heat away outside.

Warm "condenser water" from the chiller is cooled by evaporation on the roof, then sent back to the chiller to do it again.

Why it mattersIt's how the heat the chiller pulls out of the building actually leaves the building.

Cooling · economizer

Free-Cooling HX & Pump

Cools the building without the chiller.

When it's cold outside, the tower makes condenser water cold enough on its own. The free-cooling pump sends it through a plate heat exchanger that chills the chilled-water loop directly — compressors off.

Why it mattersHuge energy savings all winter — and it's why a free-cooling pump and HX sit next to the chiller on your equipment list.

Air · the hub

AHU — Air Handling Unit

Conditions and delivers the air.

Pulls in air, filters it, runs it across the chilled-water and hot-water coils to set the temperature, and a big fan pushes it to the spaces. In labs it usually runs 100% outside air.

It carries hardwired safeties that stop the fan without asking the BMS: a freezestat, a high-static cutout, and a low-static cutout.

Why it mattersIt's where the water side meets the air side — and the unit you'll watch most.

Air · life-safety

LEF — Lab Exhaust Fan

Safely removes lab air.

Roof-mounted fans pull contaminated air from fume hoods and labs and discharge it high above the building so it disperses. Usually a backup fan is ready to take over.

Why it mattersLife-safety — it keeps hazardous air moving out and labs at the right pressure.

Air · support spaces

GEF — General Exhaust Fan

Clears the rooms nobody thinks about.

Pulls air out of restrooms, janitor closets, electrical and MDF rooms, elevator machine rooms, mechanical spaces, and storage — removing odor from some and heat from others. It discharges straight to the roof: no ERM, no LEF, because this air is neither hazardous nor worth recovering heat from.

Why it mattersA dead GEF is a hot electrical or MDF room — equipment throttles or trips — and smelly restrooms. Unglamorous, and a common call.

Heat · local

UH — Unit Heater

Heats a space the ductwork doesn’t reach.

A small standalone heater with its own fan, mounted high on a wall or ceiling — usually fed by the hot-water loop (sometimes electric or gas). You’ll find them in loading docks, vestibules, stairwells, garages, electrical and mechanical rooms, and penthouses. A cabinet-style version (CUH) sits at floor level in entries and lobbies.

Why it mattersTheir real job is often freeze protection — keeping sprinkler pipes, standpipes, and water lines above freezing in unconditioned spaces. A failed UH in January is a burst-pipe call waiting to happen, so they’re a cold-weather round item.

Air · efficiency

ERM — Energy Recovery Module

Recovers energy from the air leaving — all year.

Sits between the exhaust going out and the fresh air coming in. Here it’s a glycol run-around: a coil in the exhaust stream and a coil in the OA stream, tied by a pumped glycol loop that carries energy across — the two airstreams never touch.

It runs all year, and the direction flips:
• Winter — the exhaust (~72°F) is warmer than the OA (~10°F), so the glycol picks up heat from the exhaust and preheats the incoming air.
• Summer — the exhaust (~75°F) is cooler than the OA (~90°F), so the same loop runs backwards and pre-cools the incoming air.

Why it mattersConditioning 100% outside air is expensive in both seasons — the ERM cuts that load way down either way. It idles only in mild weather, when inside and outside air are about the same temperature and there is nothing to recover.

A chiller can’t just be switched on — what has to be true first A chiller is the most interlocked machine in the plant. Before it will start, a chain of permissives all have to be satisfied — and if any one is missing, the chiller simply will not run and the BMS will tell you which one. Read them in order:

1. The BMS enables it. The plant sequence has to be calling for cooling and the chiller enabled. Nothing starts until the BMS says so.
2. The chiller’s own screen tells you where it actually is. Every chiller has a local HMI — the display on the panel — and it is the machine’s own account of itself, not the BMS’s version. Read it: is the chiller in Local or Remote? Remote means it will listen to the BMS; in Local it will ignore the enable entirely. What state does it report — Off, Ready, Start Inhibit, Run, Pre-lube, Pull-down, Unloading, Shutting Down? Is there an active alarm or a lockout waiting to be reset at the panel? And which permissive is it still waiting on? The chiller will name the missing one for you. Time spent at that screen saves an hour of guessing.
3. Condenser water flow is proven. The cooling-tower water that carries heat away — pumped by the condenser-water pump from the tower basin — has to be moving, proven by a flow switch. No CW flow, no start: the chiller would have nowhere to send its heat.
4. Chilled water flow is proven. The building water the chiller actually cools — pumped by the chilled-water pump around the loop to the AHU coils and back — has to be moving too, proven by its own flow switch. No CHW flow and the water in the barrel would freeze solid.
5. Oil temperature is up. The compressor’s oil has to be warm enough before it will start — a heater keeps it warm while the machine is idle. Start it cold and the oil is too thick to protect the bearings. This is why a chiller left with its oil heater off overnight will refuse to start in the morning.
6. No safety is tripped. Low refrigerant temperature, high condenser pressure, low oil pressure, motor overload — any one holds the start.

The pattern: a chiller needs somewhere to send heat (CW flow), something to cool (CHW flow), and a protected compressor (warm oil) before it will run. Miss any one and it stays off — on purpose.
While it runs — read it at the chiller’s own screen The HMI carries readings the BMS graphic often never shows: refrigerant pressures and temperatures, oil pressure and temperature, %RLA, and the approach temperatures. Two of them are worth watching every round.

Oil temperature and oil pressure. The compressor lives or dies on its oil. Watch that oil temperature holds in range and oil pressure stays up while it runs — a falling oil pressure is the machine telling you to look now, not later.

Approach temperature. This is the tell-tale for a fouling chiller. Approach is the gap between the water leaving a barrel and the refrigerant temperature in it — how well heat is actually crossing the tubes. As the tubes foul with scale or mud, that gap widens. A slowly climbing approach, at the same load, is an early warning that the condenser or evaporator tubes need cleaning — visible on the trend long before the chiller starts struggling to hold setpoint.
04

A peek downstream — the tenant side

Where the base building hands off

The supply air we make travels through ductwork to terminal units in each space. These are small boxes that trim the air to each zone's setpoint — and here's the key link back to the plant: their coils run on the same hot and chilled water the AHU uses. They're just smaller coils, closer to the room.

VAV box — Variable Air Volume

reheat coil only

Varies how much supply air a zone gets. Inside is a damper driven by an actuator, and an airflow sensor so the controller knows the actual CFM — not just the damper position.

A VAV box has no cooling coil — it cools by letting in more cold air. The room’s heat leaves with that air: out the exhaust in a lab, back to the AHU in an office.

Cooling: the room warms up, the damper opens, more 55°F air arrives.
Heating: the damper closes to its minimum airflow (needed for ventilation) and the reheat coil warms that air with the boiler’s hot water.

Some are cooling-only, with no coil at all. The minimum is why the reheat penalty exists — you cooled that air, now you’re heating it back up.

FPT — Fan Powered Terminal

reheat coil + CHW coil (4-pipe)

Like a VAV box, but with its own small fan. That fan pulls in return air — through a return duct or an induction opening into the ceiling plenum — and blends it with the cool primary supply air from the AHU before delivering it to the zone.

The coil isn’t always heating-only. A two-pipe unit has one coil (usually hot water); a four-pipe unit has both a hot-water and a chilled-water coil, so the same box can heat or cool the zone. Some are electric-coil instead.

Common at the perimeter, where rooms swing from cold mornings to sunny afternoons and the box has to do both.

CRAC / CRAH

recirculates room air · CHW coil

CRAC = Computer Room Air Conditioner. CRAH = Computer Room Air Handler. Both cool equipment and server rooms around the clock, independent of people-comfort cooling. Equipment doesn’t go home at 6pm — and it doesn’t tolerate a warm weekend.

The difference is where the cold comes from: a CRAH uses a chilled-water coil fed by the plant (ours). A true CRAC has its own refrigerant compressor (DX) built in. Everyone says “CRAC” for both — but they recirculate the room’s own air across a coil, which is what makes them different from the AHU.

They control humidity as well as temperature, are usually installed N+1 (a spare that can carry the load), and their alarms matter: a room full of servers heats up in minutes.

Exhaust valve / terminal

air out

The mirror image of a VAV box, on the way out. A damper or venturi valve meters exactly how much air leaves a room — that’s what makes a lab negative.

In a lab it’s paired with the supply box as a tracking pair: open a fume hood sash, the hood pulls more air, the exhaust valve opens, and the supply valve follows to hold the offset. Room stays negative at any airflow.

Lab air goes out the LEF. Restrooms and support rooms have plain exhaust grilles pulled by the GEF — no valve, no tracking, straight to the roof.

So the split is simple: our job is the plant — keep feeding clean air and water at the right temperature, pressure, and volume, and pull the used air back out. The terminals do the last few degrees in each room, and the tenant sets their thermostat. That's where the base building ends and the tenant begins.

05

Office vs. lab — one building, two different worlds

Almost every building here is part lab, part office. They share the same chillers, boilers, and pumps — but the air is handled completely differently, and mixing the two up is the most common new-hire mistake.

Office air — recirculated

the normal way

Most of the air an office breathes is the same air, used again. It returns to the AHU through the ceiling plenum or return ducts, gets mixed with a modest amount of fresh outside air (often 10–20%), is filtered and re-conditioned, and goes back out.

Cheap, because you only condition the fresh portion. Comfort-driven — the thermostat is in charge.

Lab air — once through

the expensive way

100% outside air, every time. Nothing is recirculated, because lab air may carry fumes. It’s conditioned once, used once, and exhausted through the LEF.

Expensive, and safety-driven — the hoods and exhaust decide the airflow, not the thermostat.

🔀 How they live together In a combined building the plant is shared — one chiller plant, one boiler plant, one set of pumps feeding coils on both sides. What’s separate is the air:

• A lab AHU runs 100% outside air, with the ERM upstream and the LEF pulling the exhaust out.
• An office AHU has a return air duct and a mixing box — return air plus outside air blended before the coils — and an economizer that can swing to more outside air when the weather is mild.

Some buildings put offices on their own AHU entirely; others feed office zones from the lab system (simple, but you pay lab-grade energy to cool a desk). Know which one you’re standing in before you troubleshoot.
⚠ The rule that never bends Lab air never gets recirculated into office space. Not through a duct, not through a shared return plenum, not through a propped-open door. The office is kept positive to the lab, so air always flows office → lab → exhaust, never backwards. If you ever find a return path from a lab, that’s a stop-work finding, not a work order.
🧰 What this changes for you on rounds Office complaint (“it’s too warm”) — usually a VAV box, a damper, a reheat valve, or a thermostat. Comfort problem.
Lab complaint (“the hood is alarming,” “the door whistles”) — usually airflow and pressure: exhaust valve, supply tracking, a propped door, a sash left open. Safety problem — treat it with more urgency.

Another setup — water-source heat pumps

Not every building pipes chilled and hot water from a central plant to big AHU coils. A common alternative is a water-source heat pump (WSHP) system: instead of one plant conditioning all the air, many small heat pumps around the building each heat or cool their own zone — all sharing one common water loop. The heat pumps handle temperature; separate units bring in the fresh air, and exhaust takes it back out.

Water loop Fluid Cooleror tower + HX + Boileradds if short + Loop Pumpscirculate trim loop
~60–90°F⇄ Common Water Loop tap⇄ WSHP units heat / cool→ Zones
Ventilation Outside Air temper→ HV / MAU fresh air→ Zones stale out→ General + Lab Exhaust out→ Atmosphere

The short version: two things run in parallel. The loop moves heat — cooling zones push heat into it, heating zones pull heat out, and the fluid cooler and boiler only trim the loop temperature. Meanwhile the ventilation side handles fresh air: an HV or MAU (make-up air unit) brings in and tempers outside air — because the heat pumps only recirculate zone air — and exhaust (general, plus lab exhaust) pulls the stale air back out. The MAU literally "makes up" the air the exhaust removes, which also sets the building's pressure.

What's on the common water loop

Loop pumps

circulate

Keep the loop water moving past every heat pump, around the clock.

Fluid cooler / tower

reject

Sheds the loop's excess heat outdoors when the building is net-cooling. A closed-circuit "fluid cooler" keeps the loop water clean.

Heat exchanger

isolate

Only if heat is rejected through an open cooling tower, or the loop ties into a central plant: a plate HX keeps the clean closed loop separate from the open / other water so the heat pumps don't foul. A closed fluid cooler avoids it.

Boiler / heat source

add

Adds heat to the loop when the building is net-heating and moving heat around isn't enough.

Expansion tank & make-up

hold

Absorbs thermal expansion and tops the loop up so it stays full and pressurized.

Water treatment

protect

Keeps the loop water clean so the heat pumps and piping don't scale or foul.

♻ Where it fits in a lab building You'll often see both setups in one life-science building: central AHUs handle the big 100% outside-air loads the labs need, while WSHPs handle office and support areas — with an HV / MAU (or DOAS) supplying their ventilation air and exhaust balancing it. The payoff is heat recovery: heat pulled out of a warm zone warms a cold one through the loop, instead of being thrown away and re-made.

09

Going deeper — building pressure & energy balance

ADVANCED · READ AFTER THE QUIZ

Pressure and energy are the same story told twice. Every cubic foot of air you move has a pressure consequence and an energy bill. This section explains both — and the field symptoms that come straight out of them.

⚖ The one law underneath everything — mass balance Air in must equal air out. Always. You never set building pressure directly; you set airflows, and pressure is what the building does in response.
supply + infiltration = exhaust + exfiltration
In a 100% outside-air building that becomes: AHU supply = LEF exhaust + GEF exhaust + exfiltration. Read that again — the exhaust sets the supply. Fume hoods and lab exhaust are the boss; the AHU exists to replace what the LEF throws away. That's why lab HVAC is exhaust-driven and an office building is supply-driven.
🏢 The building vs. outside — hold it slightly positive A building is normally kept +0.02 to +0.05 in. w.c. positive to outdoors: supply is deliberately a little greater than total exhaust, and the surplus leaks out. Why: no infiltration (unconditioned, unfiltered, humid or freezing air sneaking in), doors behave, and dust and odor stay out. Go negative and you get winter drafts on the lower floors and a clammy lobby in August.
Cascade

Zone pressure — air always flows clean → dirty

Each room gets a deliberate supply/exhaust imbalance. The differentials are small — 0.01–0.05 in. w.c. — but enough to hold a direction at the door gap.

How it's actually controlled: a lab isn't given a pressure setpoint — it's given an offset. Exhaust runs, say, 10% or a fixed 100–200 CFM higher than supply. Open a fume hood sash and the hood pulls more air → the lab exhaust valve opens → the supply valve tracks it to hold the same offset → the room stays negative at whatever airflow it lands on. That's a tracking pair, and it's why lab VAV boxes come in matched supply/exhaust sets.

Air changes per hour — the number behind the airflow

“6 ACH” means the room’s whole volume of air is replaced six times an hour. It is a dilution measure: how fast whatever is in the air gets carried away and replaced with clean air. Convert it to airflow like this:

CFM = ACH × room volume (ft³) ÷ 60

Work one: a lab 30 ft × 20 ft × 10 ft = 6,000 ft³. At 8 ACH → 6,000 × 8 ÷ 60 = 800 CFM of supply air, continuously. Flip it around and you can size the other way: a box delivering 800 CFM into that room is 8 ACH.

⚖ Which number actually governs — ACH or the hoods? A lab has two independent demands for air, and the larger one wins:

1. The ACH requirement — enough air to dilute the room.
2. The hood exhaust — whatever the fume hoods are pulling right now, plus the room’s negative offset.

Open every sash in a hood-heavy lab and the hoods can demand far more than 8 ACH would ever have called for — so the supply must rise to match, and the hoods are governing. In a lab with few hoods, ACH governs and the airflow just sits there. This is why sash management is an energy program, not a housekeeping nag: closing sashes lets the airflow fall back to the ACH floor.

The connection to the energy formulas below: ACH sets CFM, CFM sets the energy. Cutting a lab from 8 ACH to 4 ACH at night halves the airflow — and roughly halves the conditioning energy for those hours. No setpoint change comes close.

The energy side — same air, different lens

Three formulas explain the entire cost of a lab building. For standard air:

Sensible heat

temperature
1.08 × CFM × ΔT°F

Heating or cooling the air's temperature. This is preheat, cooling coil sensible load, and reheat.

Latent heat

moisture
0.68 × CFM × Δgrains

Adding or removing moisture. In a humid summer, wringing water out of outside air can cost more than cooling it.

Total heat

enthalpy
4.5 × CFM × Δh

Both at once, using enthalpy (Btu/lb). What the coil actually sees.

🔢 Work one — why 100% OA is expensive 50,000 CFM of outside air on a 10°F design day, warmed to a 55°F supply — a 45°F rise:
1.08 × 50,000 × 45 = 2,430,000 Btu/h
That's roughly 2.4 million Btu/h of preheat, continuously — and none of it has heated a single room yet. It only got the air to neutral. Summer is worse, because you pay the latent bill on top. Every CFM you exhaust is a CFM of outside air you must fully condition. That single sentence is the economics of lab HVAC.
♻ Where you get some back The ERM is the rebate — in both seasons. Glycol run-around coils recover roughly 40–60% of the sensible energy — and no latent, because the airstreams never mix (which is exactly why labs use them). It preheats in winter and pre-cools in summer; only in mild shoulder weather is there nothing to recover. On the example above, recovering half is over a million Btu/h you don’t buy.

Airflow is the biggest lever — not temperature. Energy is linear in CFM. Dropping lab air changes from 6 ACH occupied to 4 ACH unoccupied at night cuts a third of the load for those hours. Sash management does the same. This dwarfs any setpoint tweak.

The reheat penalty. Air is cooled to ~55°F at the AHU, then a VAV/FPT reheat coil warms it back up for a zone that didn't need that much cooling — you paid twice. That's why supply-air-temperature reset and low VAV minimums matter.

⚠ The interlock that must never be defeated If lab exhaust fails, supply must reduce or shut down too. A lab with supply and no exhaust goes positive and pushes contaminated air out into the corridor — exactly backwards. This is why the LEF sits on emergency power, and why "lab exhaust fails safe" appears on the life-safety side as well.
🧠 The four things to remember 1. Pressure is airflow's shadow. You never adjust pressure — you adjust CFM, and pressure follows.
2. Exhaust rules the lab building. Supply exists to replace what exhaust removes.
3. Clean flows to dirty. Positive to outside; corridors positive to labs; labs and restrooms negative.
4. Every exhausted CFM is a paid-for CFM. The ERM buys some back; reducing airflow buys back more.

A caveat worth stating plainly: the offsets, ACH rates, and differential setpoints above are typical ranges to build intuition. The real numbers for each room come from the building's sequences of operation and the tenant's EH&S requirements. Pull those before you teach — or change — a specific value.
