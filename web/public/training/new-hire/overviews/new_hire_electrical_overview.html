How Electrical Works in a Life-Science BuildingUtility in, stepped down, distributed — and never allowed to fail

Everything in the building — the chillers, the fans, the labs, the lights — runs on the electrical system. Utility power comes in at high voltage, gets stepped down by transformers, and is split by switchgear and panels into smaller and smaller circuits until it reaches each load. And because a lab building can't just go dark, the system is built in layers: normal power, generator-backed power, and UPS-protected power for what can't blink even for a second.

01

How it fits together

Power flows from big to small. Read each track left to right — the same loads can sit on different layers depending on how important they are.

The short version: the utility delivers power at high voltage; a transformer steps it down to 480V for the building. The main switchgear splits it through feeder breakers to panels and motor control centers, whose branch breakers feed the loads — 480V for motors, 277V for lighting, and 120V receptacles through small step-down transformers. When the utility drops, the generator starts and energizes the emergency bus; the ATSs hanging on that bus transfer their loads over in seconds. Anything that can't ride even those seconds — lab instruments, the BMS, servers — sits behind a UPS, whose batteries carry it through the gap.

🔁 How the generator, emergency bus & ATSs work together Each ATS has two inputs and one output: normal power on one side, the emergency (secondary) bus on the other, and its loads on the output. In normal life every ATS sits on the utility side and the generator is off. When an ATS senses the utility drop, it sends the start signal; the generator comes up to speed and voltage and builds the emergency bus. Each ATS then transfers its loads onto that bus — life safety first (code requires it live within seconds), standby loads after. One generator bus can carry several ATSs, each with its own priority. When the utility returns and holds steady, the ATSs retransfer back after a time delay, and the generator runs a few minutes unloaded to cool down, then stops — back to normal.
⚠ Arc flash — the rule that keeps you alive Electrical gear can release explosive energy in a fault — an arc flash. The rule for a new engineer is simple: you don't open, reset, or work in energized gear until you're trained and authorized. Respect the arc-flash labels on every panel (they state the hazard and required PPE), keep panel covers on and doors closed, and treat every conductor as live until LOTO proves otherwise. Your LOTO competency is exactly this.
⚡ Why the generator test matters That weekly generator test on your plant log is this whole second track being proven. A generator that hasn't run is a generator you can't trust — the test confirms it starts, takes load, and the ATSs will have real power to transfer to when the grid drops.
02

The seven pieces of building electrical

Protect

Breakers

The protection at every level.

A hierarchy: the main breaker at the service, feeder breakers in the switchgear, branch breakers in every panel. Each trips on overload or fault to protect the wiring downstream of it.

Why it mattersA tripped breaker is telling you something — investigate, reset once, and never hold one in against a fault.

Service

Utility Service & Transformer

Brings power in and steps it down.

The utility feed arrives at medium voltage; transformers step it down to 480V — the building's working voltage. Everything downstream hangs off this.

Why it mattersIt's the single source for the whole building — and utility-owned gear you don't touch.

Distribute

Main Switchgear

The building's electrical front door.

The main breakers and metering. It splits the service into the big feeders and is where the whole building can be shut down or isolated.

Why it mattersKnow where it is and what it feeds — and stay out of it until trained.

Distribute

Panels & Step-Down Transformers

Split power into smaller circuits.

Distribution panels feed floors and systems; dry-type transformers drop 480V to 208/120V for receptacle panels. Every circuit has a breaker and (ideally) a label.

Why it mattersTracing a dead outlet or tripped breaker starts with knowing the panel tree.

Motors

MCCs & VFDs

Start, protect, and speed-control the motors.

MCC = Motor Control Center  ·  VFD = Variable Frequency Drive

Motor control centers gather the starters for pumps and fans in one place; VFDs vary motor speed so equipment sips power at part load instead of running flat out.

Why it mattersMost of your mechanical equipment is really an electrical load — this is where it's controlled.

Backup

Generator & ATS

Keeps the building alive in an outage.

ATS = Automatic Transfer Switch

On power loss the generator auto-starts and energizes the emergency bus; each ATS (two inputs — normal & emergency — one output) transfers its loads onto it, life safety first, then standby loads like lab exhaust and freezers. Retransfers back when the utility returns and holds.

Why it mattersLife safety and the science both depend on this working — hence the weekly test.

Critical

UPS

Bridges the gap the generator can't.

UPS = Uninterruptible Power Supply

A generator takes seconds to start; a UPS rides through on batteries instantly, keeping lab instruments, the BMS, and servers from ever seeing the blink — and conditioning the power besides.

Why it mattersSome experiments and systems can't recover from even a one-second drop.

03

The life-science layer — lab power

The extra electrical a lab building needs

Labs hold work that can't lose power — years of samples in a −80°C freezer, running experiments, exhaust that must never stop. So the lab side gets its own electrical layer on top of the building systems above.

Red outlets

standby power

Red receptacles are on generator-backed circuits — freezers and critical equipment plug in there, so they ride through an outage.

EPO — emergency power off

safety

Big red EPO buttons kill power to a lab or room instantly in an emergency. Know where they are — and don't lean on them.

Freezer & equipment alarms

monitor

Critical freezers and equipment are monitored and alarmed — a power or temperature problem calls someone before the science is lost.

So the split is simple: building electrical gets power in, steps it down, and distributes it; the lab layer decides which loads ride the generator, which get a UPS, and how a problem gets noticed at 2 AM. When a breaker trips or an outage hits, knowing which layer a load sits on tells you how urgent it is.

Why is the generator running?

“The generator is running.” That, by itself, is not information.

A running generator tells you almost nothing until you know whether the ATS transferred. The engine is not the diagnostic — the ATS position is. There are ten reasons the set may be running, and in six of them the building is completely normal.

Before you stop a running generator — read five things, in this order 1. The ATS — Automatic Transfer Switch. Normal, or Emergency? If it reads Emergency, that generator is the building’s power. Read it at the switch, not on a BMS graphic. The graphic shows a point somebody mapped; the ATS shows what the contacts did.
  • The controller screen, on newer units: source status, present position, and any timer counting down.
  • The indicating lights. Normal Source Available · Emergency Source Available · Load on Normal · Load on Emergency. Two of them tell you what power exists; two tell you where the load is. They are not the same question.
  • The Not-In-Auto lamp. If it is lit, that switch will not transfer by itself. It is the single most important light on the door.
  • The mechanical position indicator on the transfer mechanism — the flag or pointer. Contacts do not lie.
  • On a bypass-isolation ATS, also read the bypass handle and the isolation handle: is the switch carrying the load, or is bypass carrying it while the switch sits isolated?
  • Read it from the front, through the window and off the labels. Do not open the door. The arc-flash label on that enclosure governs, and it is not a door you open to satisfy curiosity.
2. The utility. Volts on all three legs, or not?
3. Who started it — and was it you? The person who starts a generator is the person who stops it. If you did not start it, you do not stop it. Find whoever did: the technician, the vendor, the on-call engineer, the supervisor who authorized the test. That machine is running because somebody put it there for a reason, and you do not yet know the reason.
4. The controller mode. AUTO · exercise running · cool-down timer counting · not in AUTO.
5. The schedule and the work orders. Is this the weekly exercise, or an annual test somebody posted?

Only then decide. You stop a running generator for exactly one reason: a genuine emergency in front of you — fire, mechanical failure, someone injured. In that moment you are choosing to drop emergency power, knowingly.
Why this is the trap it is During a no-load exercise the engine runs and the building is perfectly normal — that is exactly what the test is. So “everything looks fine, therefore the generator should not be running” is a backwards inference, and it is the reasoning that gets a generator shut down mid-test.

The cost is real. Stop it during an outage and you drop egress lighting, the fire alarm, the fire pump, elevator recall, and lab exhaust — which fails safe onto emergency power precisely so containment survives a blackout — along with freezers and cold rooms. Stop it during a cool-down and you shut a hot turbocharged diesel down without its unloaded run; oil cokes in the turbo bearing. It will not fail today. It will fail on the night you need it. And if you use the E-stop, the set is now in lockout and needs a manual reset at the panel — so the generator that merely looked wrong is now genuinely unavailable.
