How Plumbing Works in a Life-Science BuildingWater in, hot water, waste out — in plain terms

A life-science building uses water for far more than restrooms — sinks, safety showers, humidifiers, cooling make-up, and lab process water — and it has to carry away waste that can include corrosive lab chemicals. Plumbing is the set of systems that bring clean water in, heat some of it, push it everywhere it's needed, and take the waste out — all while protecting the city's drinking water from anything hazardous inside the building. It also runs two grades of water on separate piping: potable (safe to drink) and non-potable (fine for machines, not for people).

The short version: the city delivers cold water through a meter; a backflow preventer stops anything in the building from flowing back into the public supply. A PRV steps the pressure down and a booster pump raises it for the upper floors. Some of that cold water is heated and kept circulating in a hot loop so it's hot at every tap. Non-potable water comes two ways: from non-potable sources (reclaimed, grey, rain), or from potable water that crosses an RPZ (reduced pressure zone backflow preventer) into a mechanical system — the moment it does, it's non-potable. That's how the chilled, heating, and WSHP (water-source heat pump) loops, the RO/DI feed, and (in many buildings) the tower make-up get their water. After it's used, waste drains through P-trapped piping (each drain sealed by a U of standing water) to the sewer, while a vent stack runs up to the roof so air can get in and sewer gas out — without vents, drains gurgle and traps siphon dry. And rain never joins the waste: roof drains (with overflow backups) feed rain leaders down to the city storm sewer on completely separate piping — or into capture for non-potable reuse. One more special case: garage and loading-dock drains run through an oil / water separator — the oil floats, gets trapped, and is pumped out; only the water continues to the sanitary sewer.

🚰 Potable vs non-potable — two separate systems Potable water is the treated drinking water that feeds sinks, showers, safety showers — anything people touch (that's the cold and hot tracks above). Non-potable water is fine for machines but not for people: reclaimed municipal water, grey water, or captured rain / condensate, used for cooling-tower make-up, toilet flushing, and irrigation. The two are never cross-connected — non-potable pipe is marked and labeled so it is never mistaken for potable, and backflow preventers guard the potable side. Knowing which pipe is which is a real safety habit.
🛡 Backflow protection — keeping the city water clean In a lab building the biggest plumbing risk is a cross-connection — lab chemicals, process water, or equipment siphoning back into the drinking water if pressure drops. Backflow preventers (RPZ — reduced pressure zone assemblies) at the service and at every high-hazard connection block that. They're a code item, tested every year, and one of the first things you'll learn to spot.
♨ Recirculation & Legionella Hot water is kept moving in a recirc loop so it's hot at every fixture instead of sitting warm in dead legs — the warm, still water where Legionella grows. Mixing valves then temper it down to a safe delivery temperature at the tap. Storing and circulating hot enough, delivering warm enough: that balance is a real part of the job.

What the water feeds — and which grade

Whether a connection is potable or non-potable depends on what it serves — and any tie into a mechanical system is a cross-connection that needs a backflow preventer (RPZ).

Sinks · showers · safety fixtures

potable

Anything people drink or touch is potable, including safety showers and eyewash — no exceptions.

Cooling tower make-up

either grade

Big user (replacing evaporation). Fed by reclaimed / non-potable where available — or directly from potable through a PRV + RPZ.

RO / DI feed

potable + RPZ

Potable water feeds the purifier — through its own RPZ, since it’s a high-hazard connection. (What comes out is the lab-water system, covered below.)

Heating & chilled-water loops

potable + RPZ

Small top-up for the closed loops — but once inside, the water (with chemicals / glycol) is non-potable. The RPZ keeps it from ever getting back.

WSHP condenser loop

potable + RPZ

WSHP = water-source heat pump. Same as the loops above — a small potable make-up, protected by an RPZ so loop water can't back up into the potable supply.

Toilet & urinal flushing

non-potable

A prime reclaimed / non-potable use wherever such a system exists — no reason to flush with drinking water.

Irrigation

non-potable

Landscaping runs on reclaimed / non-potable water, keeping the potable supply for people.

02

The seven pieces of building plumbing

Protect

Backflow Preventer (RPZ)

Protects the city's drinking water.

Stops water inside the building — lab chemicals, boiler water, irrigation — from siphoning back into the public main if pressure ever reverses.

Why it mattersIt's a health-and-safety device, tested annually — the line between the building and everyone's drinking water.

Service

Water Meter & Service Entrance

Where the water comes in and is measured.

The service main, the main shutoff, and the meter — the single point the whole building's water passes through.

Why it mattersKnow where the main shutoff is before you ever need it.

Pressure

PRV — Pressure-Reducing Valve

Steps the incoming pressure down.

City pressure is often too high for the building's fixtures; the PRV drops it to a steady, safe level the fixtures are built for.

Why it mattersToo much pressure wears out valves and fixtures and causes water hammer — the bang you hear when fast-moving water is stopped suddenly by a closing valve and slams the pipe.

Pressure

Domestic Booster Pump

Raises pressure for the upper floors.

Water loses pressure going up a tall building; the booster keeps flow strong at the top floors, usually with a backup pump and pressure control.

Why it mattersWithout it, upper-floor fixtures and safety showers would trickle.

Hot water

Water Heater & Recirc

Makes hot water and keeps it moving.

Heats the domestic water and circulates it in a loop so it's hot at every fixture — which also keeps it out of the warm range where Legionella grows.

Why it mattersComfort, hygiene, and Legionella control all ride on this loop.

Drainage

Sanitary Waste & Vent

Carries waste away and lets the system breathe.

Every drain has a P-trap — a U of standing water that seals the pipe and blocks sewer gas; vents let air in so the drains flow freely. Below-grade fixtures use a sump / ejector pump to lift waste up to the sewer, and garage / loading-dock drains pass through an oil / water separator first — oil never goes to the sewer (and never to storm).

Why it mattersA dry trap or blocked vent means sewer gas or slow, gurgling drains.

Drainage

Roof & Storm Drainage

Gets rain off the roof — on its own system.

Roof drains (plus overflow drains as backup) feed rain leaders that drop through the building to the city storm sewer. Storm and sanitary are two separate systems — rain never joins the waste — and captured rain / condensate can feed the non-potable side. The field rule: rain and only rain → storm; touched anything else → sanitary, with treatment (separator, neutralization) on the way if needed.

Why it mattersA clogged roof drain ponds water on the roof — real weight, real leaks. Check them on rounds, especially after storms and leaf season.

03

The life-science layer — lab plumbing

The extra plumbing a lab building needs

A life-science building puts things down the drain — and needs water — that a normal office never does: corrosive chemicals, ultra-pure process water, and emergency drench water. So there's a plumbing layer built just for the labs.

Acid / lab waste

neutralize

Corrosive lab waste runs in special acid-resistant pipe to a neutralization tank that corrects its pH — the acid/base scale, 0 (strong acid) to 14 (strong base), 7 is neutral — into the safe band the sewer code allows (roughly 6–9) before it reaches the sewer.

RO / DI lab water

purify

Reverse-osmosis / deionized systems make ultra-pure water for lab equipment on dedicated piping. Purity is read two ways — conductivity (µS/cm, lower = purer) or its inverse resistivity (MΩ·cm, higher = purer):

• Tap water: ~300–800 µS/cm
• RO water: ~5–50 µS/cm
• DI / polished: <0.1 µS/cm ≈ up to 18.2 MΩ·cm

A rising µS/cm (falling MΩ·cm) means the resin is spent.

Showers & eyewash

emergency

Drench safety showers and eyewash need tepid water fast; special mixing valves keep it in a safe temperature range.

And every one of those lab connections is a cross-connection risk, so backflow preventers guard the potable water wherever the labs tie in. Building plumbing serves the whole building; the lab layer serves the hazards — and both are watched to keep the drinking water clean.
