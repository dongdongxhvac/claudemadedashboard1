CW-1

Chiller — start sequence & interlocks

Components

evaporator / condenser barrelscompressor (centrifugal or screw)oil system: pump, heater, coolerVFD or inlet guide vanesstarter / soft startflow switchesmotorized CHW (evaporator) isolation valve + end switchmotorized CW (condenser) isolation valve + end switchBAS interlock chain

Controls & sequences

  • It starts at the BMS. Before any start, restart, or operational check: read the graphic. Mode and schedule · the alarm list · every permissive point · command vs status on each interlock · anything left in Hand. You walk to the machine with a hypothesis, not a flashlight (Rule 1)
  • Permissives before start: CHW and CW isolation valves proven open on their end switches → CHW pump proven → CW pump proven → tower enabled → flow switches made → oil temp above minimum → no active safety
  • Isolation valves come first, and they are slow. On a headered plant each chiller has a motorized valve on the evaporator and one on the condenser. They stroke in 60–120 seconds, so the sequence waits on the end switch before a pump is ever commanded. Flow proof alone is not enough: a flow switch can make on water going the wrong way through an idle machine
  • Their real job is to stop bypass. An idle chiller with a leaking valve lets return water short-circuit through a barrel that is not cooling anything. The plant blends warm and cold, leaving-water temperature rises, the running chiller loads up, and you pay to pump water through a machine that is off. This is a large part of low delta-T syndrome
  • Shutdown is the reverse, with a delay. Compressor off → pumps keep running through the coast-down so residual heat leaves the barrel → then the valves close. Never close an isolation valve on a running chiller — the valves are interlocked to chiller status for exactly that reason
  • Start sequence: valves proven → pumps proven → oil pump prelube → compressor start → vanes/VFD ramp on leaving-CHW setpoint → load limit during pull-down
  • Head pressure / lift control: minimum condenser-water temperature for start; tower bypass holds CW warm enough to keep the machine off surge and the oil pressure up
  • Approach temperatures: evaporator and condenser approach as fouling indicators
  • Safeties: low evap refrigerant temp, high condenser pressure, low oil pressure, motor overload, low chilled-water flow

Operator tasks

  • Log amps, %RLA, evap/cond pressures & temps, approach, oil pressure & temp
  • Confirm the interlock chain before any manual start
  • Stroke and prove every isolation valve during the shutdown season — watch the blade or the stem, not the actuator
  • Feel an idle chiller’s barrels. A cold or warming evaporator on an off machine means its valve is leaking through
  • Never bypass a flow switch

How it fails

  • Surge (centrifugal): low lift or fouled condenser
  • Isolation valve leaking through on an idle chiller → bypass, blended water, low delta-T, wasted pumping
  • End switch made while the blade is still shut → pump deadheaded, evaporator at risk of freezing
  • Valve commanded closed before the machine finished coasting down
  • Nuisance trips from a failing flow switch
  • Oil heater off overnight → start blocked
CW-2

Chilled-water distribution & DP control

Components

primary pumpssecondary / distribution pumps on VFDsdecoupler (bypass) lineDP sensor at the hydraulically remote pointtwo-way control valves at coils

Controls & sequences

  • Differential-pressure control: secondary pump VFDs modulate to hold DP setpoint at the remote sensor — not at the pump
  • DP reset: setpoint slides down until the most-open valve is nearly wide open. Big energy lever
  • Decoupler flow direction tells you production vs demand: reverse flow means secondary is pulling more than the chillers make
  • Low delta-T syndrome: causes (valves stuck open, fouled coils, three-way valves, low load) and why it forces extra chillers on

Operator tasks

  • Read DP setpoint and actual; check pump speeds
  • Watch decoupler flow / temperature
  • Trend delta-T across the plant

How it fails

  • Delta-T collapse → chillers run at part load, kW/ton rises
  • DP sensor drift → whole system over-pumps
CW-3

Cooling tower operation

Components

fill mediahot deck / distribution basinspray nozzlesdrift eliminatorsfan + VFD or two-speed motorgearbox / drive shaftvibration switch on the fan/gearboxcold-water basin & sumpsump heaterheat trace on make-up and basin pipingbasin sweeper piping

Controls & sequences

  • Water temperature control has two stages, and the valve is the first one. To hold the condenser-water supply setpoint the sequence works a tower bypass valve first, then the fans. The valve blends: a three-way (or a pair of two-way valves) splits CW return between over the tower and straight through the bypass. Cold day, light load → most flow bypasses, fans off. As load rises → the valve sends more water over the fill, then the fan VFD modulates, then fans stage. Leading with the valve avoids thermally shocking the chiller and stops the fans short-cycling
  • Where the water goes. Warm CW return is pumped up to the hot deck / distribution basin on top, falls through the spray nozzles across the fill, and collects cooled in the cold-water basin; the CWP draws from the basin back to the condenser. Drift eliminators catch droplets before they leave. On a multi-cell tower, equalizer lines tie the basins together so cells share level, and each cell’s isolation valves let you run some cells and idle others with load
  • Flow has to stay in its lane. Nozzles need a minimum flow to spray an even pattern — too little and you get dry spots in the fill; too much and the deck floods and short-circuits. Idle cells still need their return valve shut or they steal flow from the working cells and overflow at the basin
  • Why the bypass protects the chiller. Push CW too cold and a centrifugal chiller loses the lift it needs — head pressure collapses, oil pressure with it, and the machine surges or trips. The bypass keeping CW warm enough in cold weather is condenser-water control and chiller protection in one loop
  • Water level control: make-up valve on float or conductivity/level probes; overflow, drain, and equalizer lines between cells
  • Freeze protection: sump heater with the tower idle; heat trace on exposed piping; basin heater thermostat and low-limit alarm
  • Vibration switch: a hardwired cutout on the fan/gearbox. A thrown blade, a failing bearing, or an ice-loaded fan trips it and stops the fan before it tears itself off the deck. Like the other safeties it is manual reset — you go and find out why
  • Winter operation: fans off / reversed, bypass in service, basin heated

Operator tasks

  • Verify spray nozzle pattern and even flow across the deck; look for clogged or missing nozzles
  • Confirm the bypass valve strokes fully both ways and that CW supply actually follows it
  • Check basin level, sweep the strainer, and confirm idle-cell isolation valves are shut
  • Test the vibration switch and know where its reset is
  • Confirm sump heater and heat trace energized before first freeze
  • Log CW supply/return, fan speed, bypass valve position, and approach to wet bulb

How it fails

  • Clogged nozzles → dry spots in the fill → poor heat transfer
  • Bypass stuck closed → CW too cold → chiller loses lift → surge or oil-pressure trip
  • Bypass stuck open → CW too warm → chiller can’t hold setpoint, head pressure climbs
  • Idle cell’s valve left open → flow robbed from working cells, basin overflow
  • Vibration switch bypassed → a failing fan runs to destruction
  • Sump heater failed in November → frozen basin

CW-6

Corrosion & microbiological monitoring

Components

coupon rack: pre-weighed mild-steel and copper couponsflow meter / rotameter on the rackisolation valvesdip slidessample ports

Controls & sequences

  • Coupons are exposed for a set period (commonly ~90 days) at a controlled velocity (typically ~3–5 ft/s) so results are comparable
  • Result is reported in mils per year (mpy) — thousandths of an inch of metal lost per year
  • Uniform loss vs pitting matters more than the number: a low mpy with deep pits is worse than a higher uniform rate
  • Open condenser loop and closed loops are monitored separately — different chemistry, different targets

Operator tasks

  • Record install date, remove on schedule, don't touch the coupon with bare hands
  • Verify the rack has flow — a dry rack gives a meaningless result
  • File the lab report with the treatment vendor's monthly

How it fails

  • Rack isolated and forgotten → six months of nothing
  • Coupons reused or mishandled → invalid weight loss

CW-8

Closed loops — chilled & heating water

Components

Controls & sequences

AH-1

AHU anatomy & the start / stop sequence

Components

OA damper + end switchERM coil (OA side)filter banks + DP switchCHW coilHHW / preheat coilsupply fan + VFDreturn or relief fanfan status proof: current switch or DP switchaccess-door safety switchfreezestatduct smoke detectorhigh-static cutoutlow-static cutout

Controls & sequences

  • It starts at the BMS. Before any start, restart, or operational check: read the graphic. Mode and schedule · the alarm list · every permissive point · command vs status on each interlock · anything left in Hand. You walk to the machine with a hypothesis, not a flashlight (Rule 1)
  • Start permissives: smoke detector clear · freezestat not tripped · high-static cutout not tripped · low-static (negative) cutout not tripped · access doors closed · OA and discharge dampers proven open on their end switches. A fan started against a closed damper destroys ductwork
  • The access-door safety switch is a permissive, not a courtesy. On many units, opening an AHU door drops the fan and blocks a start. It is hardwired, and it exists because people go inside these boxes. It is not a substitute for LOTO — it is the thing that catches you when LOTO was skipped
  • Nothing is released to control until the fan is proven. The fan is commanded, then proven by an independent device — a current switch, or a differential-pressure switch across the fan. Command is not proof (Rule 4)
  • The release order matters. Prove fan status → hold the VFD at minimum speed through a proving delay (typically 30–120 s, per the sequence) → then release the duct-static / speed loop → then enable the SAT loop and the coil valves
  • Why the valves wait: a coil must never be heated or chilled with no air moving across it. Until the fan is proven, valves sit at their fail-safe position. Release the valve loop early and you can freeze a coil, or boil one, with the fan still coasting up
  • Why the speed loop waits: released too early, the static-pressure PID sees no pressure, winds up, and slams the fan toward 100% the moment air starts moving
  • The two static safeties are a matched pair. The high-limit sits in the supply duct and stops the fan before it bursts the ductwork — a closed damper, a shut fire damper, or every VAV box driving closed. The low-limit sits in the return or exhaust duct and stops the fan before it implodes it — a blocked return, a jammed relief damper, or a stuck return fan. Both are hardwired, manual-reset switches; the BMS is told, it is not asked
  • Ramp: fan starts at minimum speed and rises under static-pressure control, not straight to command
  • Shutdown: fan stops, dampers close, and in cold weather the preheat and ERM glycol pump may keep running to protect coils
  • Every one of those safeties is hardwired. They act whether or not the BMS agrees

Operator tasks

  • Read the sequence of operation before touching an AHU
  • Confirm damper end switches actually make — do not trust the graphic
  • Verify the door switch drops the fan; never tape, block or jumper one
  • Trend the start: command → status → speed → static → valve output. The order tells you whether the sequence is really running as written
  • Log filter DP; a loaded filter starves the box even while the fan reads ‘running’

How it fails

  • Fan commanded on, damper never opened → burst duct on the supply side, or a collapsed return duct on the low-limit side
  • A static safety repeatedly reset instead of investigated — the cause is still there
  • Door switch jumpered so the unit runs with a door open — the one safety that protects a person inside
  • Valve loop released before fan proof → coil heated or chilled with no airflow
  • Static loop released before fan proof → wind-up, then the fan slams to 100%
  • Smoke detector left in test → AHU disabled and nobody knows
AH-2

Supply-air temperature control & reset

Components

CHW valveHHW valvedischarge air temperature sensormixed-air sensorSAT reset logic

Controls & sequences

  • Coils are sequenced with a deadband between them so heating and cooling never fight. If both valves are open at once, the deadband is gone or a valve is leaking by
  • Typical discharge setpoint around 55°F — but it is set by the sequence, not by tradition
  • SAT reset / trim & respond: raise the supply temperature until a zone complains, then trim back. Large energy saving, and it directly reduces reheat penalty
  • A 100% OA lab AHU has no return air to mix — the coils do all the work, every hour of the year

Operator tasks

  • Trend discharge temp, its setpoint, and both valve outputs on one chart
  • A valve at 100% for days is saturated — capacity, a lying sensor, or an impossible setpoint

How it fails

  • Both valves open together → simultaneous heating and cooling
  • Leaking CHW valve masquerading as a control problem
AH-3

Duct static pressure & fan control

Components

static pressure sensorsupply fan VFDhigh-static cutout (hardwired)VAV box dampers

Controls & sequences

  • The static sensor sits two-thirds down the duct, not at the fan. The VFD modulates to hold its setpoint
  • Static pressure reset: lower the setpoint until the most-open VAV damper is nearly wide open. The single biggest fan-energy lever
  • The high-static cutout is a hardwired safety, not a control. If it trips, something closed that should not have
  • A fan pinned at 100% speed is saturated: check filters, check dampers, check whether the sensor is reading at all

Operator tasks

  • Trend static setpoint, static actual, fan speed, and the most-open damper together
  • Never raise static setpoint to solve a hot-zone complaint before checking that zone’s box

How it fails

  • Static setpoint cranked up years ago and never reset
  • Sensor tubing pinched or disconnected — fan chases a phantom
AH-4

Outside air, economizer & ventilation

Components

OA damper & actuatorminimum-position settingairflow measuring stationreturn / relief dampersCO₂ sensors (offices)

Controls & sequences

  • Minimum OA is a ventilation requirement, not a comfort setting. It does not get closed to save energy
  • Economizer: in mild weather open the dampers wide and cool with outside air instead of the chiller. Changeover on dry bulb or enthalpy
  • A 100% OA lab AHU has no economizer — it is always at 100%. There is nothing to switch
  • Demand-controlled ventilation trims office OA on CO₂. Never applied to labs

Operator tasks

  • Verify minimum position at the airflow station, not at the actuator scale
  • Check economizer changeover each shoulder season

How it fails

  • Damper linkage loose: actuator strokes, blades stay shut
  • Economizer stuck at minimum → chiller runs on a 50°F day
AH-5

ERM — glycol run-around, in detail

Components

OA coil (inside the AHU)exhaust coil (in the exhaust duct)glycol pump3-way valveexpansion tankfreeze-point sample port

Controls & sequences

  • Two coils, one loop. The airstreams never touch — that is why labs use a run-around instead of a wheel
  • Winter: exhaust (~72°F) is warmer than OA, glycol preheats the incoming air. Summer: exhaust is cooler than OA, the same loop pre-cools it. It idles only in mild weather
  • Frost control: in deep cold the exhaust coil can frost. A 3-way valve modulates glycol around the OA coil to hold the exhaust coil above freezing — you give up some recovery to protect the coil
  • Effectiveness is typically 40–60% sensible only. No moisture is transferred

Operator tasks

  • Sample glycol annually: freeze point by refractometer, pH, inhibitor reserve
  • Confirm the pump runs whenever recovery is available — an idle ERM is money on the roof

How it fails

  • Glycol degraded to acid — pH falls, corrosion starts
  • Pump off and nobody noticed; the AHU simply works harder
AH-6

Freeze protection & the hardwired safeties — freezestat, high & low static

Components

freezestat (capillary, averaging element)high-static cutout (supply duct)low-static cutout (return / exhaust duct)preheat coilOA damper fail-closed spring returnlow-limit thermostatcoil drain / drain-downmanual reset buttons

Controls & sequences

  • What the static safeties do: they protect the ductwork from the fan — not the fan from the duct
  • Why they must exist. A VFD fan is a pressure source, not a flow source. It does not blow a fixed amount of air; it does whatever it takes to reach its static setpoint. Close the path and flow goes to zero while pressure climbs toward the fan’s shutoff head — on a typical AHU 6–10" w.c. The duct is a thin sheet-metal pressure vessel built to a construction class, often 3–4" positive and only 1–2" negative. The fan can out-pressure the duct, and nothing else in the system stops it
  • The control loop accelerates into the failure. If the static sensor sits upstream of whatever closed, it reads low pressure — so the PID calls for more speed
  • High-static cutout: positive pressure, supply duct, downstream of the fan. It prevents bursting — split seams, blown flex, damaged VAV boxes. Causes: a fire or smoke damper closed after an alarm test, a stuck isolation damper, every VAV driving closed, a blank left in the duct after work, or plugged sensing tubing making the fan chase pressure that is not there
  • Low-static cutout: negative pressure, return or exhaust duct or the fan inlet plenum. It prevents implosion. Causes: return fan not started, return damper closed, OA damper closed on a 100% OA unit, a louver packed with snow, a blocked bird screen
  • Why the low limit is a separate device. A positive failure is a seam letting go — loud, messy, survivable. A negative failure is structural buckling: the duct holds, holds, holds, then collapses flat in an instant. Ducts are rated for far less negative than positive, so a return duct or mixing plenum can implode at pressures the supply side would not notice
  • Why hardwired, why manual reset. The thing that would otherwise stop the fan is the same controller that commanded it — and that controller can be overridden, wound up, in Hand, or simply not talking. So the switch acts alone. The reset is manual because the blockage is still there: the switch did not fail, it did its job. Auto-reset would cycle the fan into the same closed damper until something gave
  • The safety trip point is not the control setpoint. Static may control around 1.0–1.5"; the safety trips well above that, and must still sit below the duct’s construction rating. Raising the control setpoint to chase a hot zone walks the operating point toward the trip — and toward the duct rating
  • A fan can burst a duct in seconds, faster than any BMS alarm-and-response loop. These switches are the fan’s equivalent of a boiler’s safety relief valve: purely mechanical, last line, never bypassed
  • The freezestat senses the coldest foot of its capillary, not the average. It trips on a cold spot. The element must be woven serpentine across the full coil face — coiled up in one corner it protects one corner
  • All three are hardwired. They act whether or not the BMS agrees, and all three are manual reset by design — so somebody has to go and look at why
  • OA dampers fail closed on power loss precisely so a dead controller cannot freeze a coil
  • When the freezestat trips: supply fan stops (and the return fan with it) → OA damper drives closed → the preheat / HHW valve drives open to flood the coil with warm water → the CHW valve closes → glycol or ERM pump keeps running → alarm at the BMS → manual reset at the device. The order is deliberate: stop the cold air first, then warm the coil
  • When the high-static cutout trips: supply fan stops, dampers close, alarm, manual reset. It sits in the supply duct and it saved the ductwork from bursting. Something closed that should not have — a shut fire damper, a stuck damper, every VAV box driving closed
  • When the low-static cutout trips: the fan stops before the return or exhaust duct implodes. Something blocked the return — a jammed relief damper, a plugged return, a stuck return fan, or a supply fan running against a closed return path
  • What all three look like on the graphic: fan commanded ON, status OFF (Rule 4). If the safety’s aux contact is not mapped to the BMS, that is all you see — a fan that will not run, and no alarm explaining it. Go and look at the reset buttons
  • A reset is a decision, not a reflex. Reset once, after you know what tripped it. A safety that trips twice is telling you something true

Operator tasks

  • Test the freezestat on schedule; know where every reset button is — freezestat, high static, low static
  • Verify the trip response, not just the trip: does the OA damper actually close and the preheat valve actually open?
  • Check that the capillary is still woven across the coil face and has not been bundled during a filter change
  • After any trip, find the cause before you reset. Log it
  • Never jumper a freezestat or a static safety, not even for an hour

How it fails

  • Frozen and burst coil — days of downtime and water damage
  • Freezestat trips repeatedly, reset each time, cause never found
  • Capillary coiled in one spot → most of the coil is unprotected
  • Static safety tubing plugged or pinched → the switch never trips, and the duct is the fuse
  • Control static setpoint raised over the years until it sits just under the trip point
  • A tripped safety with no mapped aux contact → an unexplained fan that will not start

Components

  • Lab exhaust fails safe: lose a fan and the standby starts. It never simply stops. LEFs ride emergency power

AH-8

Terminal units — VAV, FPT and box tuning

Components

VAV box: damper, actuator, airflow sensor, reheat coilFPT: fan, return path, reheat coil, CHW coil on 4-pipecontroller and thermostat

Controls & sequences

  • A VAV box has no cooling coil. It cools by admitting more 55°F air; the room’s heat leaves with that air
  • Minimum airflow is a ventilation floor. Heating happens at that minimum, through the reheat coil — hence the reheat penalty
  • The box is pressure independent: it measures actual CFM and controls to a flow setpoint, so duct pressure swings do not upset the zone
  • An FPT pulls return air with its own fan and blends it with primary air. Four-pipe units can also cool

Operator tasks

  • Check box minimums against the sequence — inflated minimums are a hidden energy cost
  • Trend zone temp, flow setpoint, actual flow, and reheat valve together

How it fails

  • Airflow sensor tubing plugged → box controls to a lie
  • Reheat valve leaking by → the box heats while calling for cooling
BO-1

Boiler start sequence & flame safeguard

Components

burner & blowergas train: safety shutoff valves, regulator, ventpilot / igniterflame scanner or rodflame safeguard controllerair-proving switchhigh & low gas pressure switchesHW pump + flow switchstack / fluecombustion-air source: louver, HV or MAU

Controls & sequences

  • It starts at the BMS. Before any start, restart, or operational check: read the graphic. Mode and schedule · the alarm list · every permissive point · command vs status on each interlock · anything left in Hand. You walk to the machine with a hypothesis, not a flashlight (Rule 1)
  • Permissives: HW pump proven, flow switch made, combustion air proven, gas pressure between the high and low switches, no safety in lockout
  • How combustion air is proven depends on the plant. A simple room has a motorized louver with an end switch. Many older, lower-efficiency plants instead depend on an HV or MAU unit that must be proven running — fan status and damper end switch — before the flame safeguard will release the boiler to fire. No air unit, no boiler. That is the interlock doing its job, not a fault to be worked around
  • Sequence: call for heat → pumps and flow proven → combustion air damper opens → pre-purge (the chamber is swept for a set number of air changes) → ignition trial → pilot proven by the scanner → main gas valve opens → main flame proven within the trial-for-ignition window → modulate to firing rate → post-purge on shutdown
  • If flame is not proven inside the trial window, the flame safeguard locks out — and lockout is manual reset by design, so a person has to come and ask why
  • Never bypass, jumper, or repeatedly reset the flame safeguard. Unburned gas in a hot chamber is how boiler rooms come apart

Operator tasks

  • Log stack temperature, firing rate, and gas pressures
  • Keep the flame scanner lens clean; a dirty scanner reads a good flame as failure
  • Record every lockout — three in a week is a fault, not bad luck

How it fails

  • Lockout reset again and again, cause never found
  • Air-proving switch tubing plugged → purge never proven
  • Dirty scanner → nuisance flame-failure trips
  • MAU or HV left off, in Hand, or on a schedule that does not match the boiler → boilers will not fire, and the call comes in as ‘no heat’
BO-2

Hot-water distribution, staging & outdoor reset

Components

primary and secondary HW pumpsVFDsdecouplertwo-way control valvesDP sensorexpansion tankair separatormake-up assembly with RPZ and meter

Controls & sequences

  • Outdoor reset: the hot-water supply setpoint slides with outside air temperature — hotter as it gets colder. The largest single energy lever on the heating plant
  • Staging and lead/lag: boilers are brought on in sequence and rotated so hours accumulate evenly. A boiler that short-cycles is being staged wrong, not failing
  • Minimum flow through the boiler must be maintained whatever the secondary loop is doing — that is what the primary loop and decoupler are for
  • The make-up meter is a leak detector. A closed loop should take almost no water

Operator tasks

  • Trend HW supply, HW return, the reset setpoint, and boiler firing rate together
  • Read the make-up meter every round and trend it
  • Check lead/lag rotation and runtime hours

How it fails

  • A reset curve installed at commissioning and never tuned
  • Short cycling on low load — wear, and terrible efficiency
  • Rising make-up quietly diluting the inhibitor to nothing
BO-3

Condensing vs non-condensing — return temperature is the whole game

Components

condensing boiler (stainless / aluminium HX)non-condensing boiler (cast iron or steel)condensate drain & neutralizerboiler bypass / blending valvereturn water temperature sensor

Controls & sequences

  • A condensing boiler only condenses — and only reaches its rated efficiency — when the return water is below the flue-gas dew point, roughly 130°F. The colder the return, the more efficient it gets. Supply temperature is not what decides this. Return temperature is
  • A non-condensing boiler needs the return kept above roughly 140°F. Let the return go cold and flue gas condenses on cold cast iron: acidic condensate, corrosion, and thermal shock
  • So the same outdoor reset that saves money on a condensing plant will destroy a non-condensing one unless a bypass or blending valve holds the return up
  • Condensate from a condensing boiler is acidic and must pass a neutralizer before the sanitary drain

Operator tasks

  • Know which type you have before you touch a reset schedule
  • Trend return water temperature, not just supply
  • Service the condensate neutralizer; check drain pH

How it fails

  • A condensing boiler run at a 150°F return — you paid for efficiency you never once used
  • A non-condensing boiler on a deep reset curve — corroding from the inside
  • Neutralizer media exhausted, acid going to drain
BO-4

Steam-to-water heat exchanger & the campus steam interface

Components

shell-and-tube or plate HXsteam PRV stationsteam control valvesteam trapcondensate receiver & pumpvacuum breakersafety relief valve

Controls & sequences

  • The control valve modulates steam to hold the hot-water supply setpoint. The building never sees steam; it sees hot water
  • The trap lets condensate out and holds steam in. A failed-open trap blows live steam to the condensate system; a failed-closed trap floods the HX and the output collapses
  • Stall: at low load the pressure in the HX can fall below the condensate return pressure, condensate stops draining, and you get water hammer. The vacuum breaker and correct trap sizing are what prevent it
  • Condensate return is metered — it is how the campus knows what you used

Operator tasks

  • Trap survey on schedule; a failed trap is invisible until the bill arrives
  • Log steam pressure, HWS temperature, and condensate return
  • Listen for hammer at low load; it is a warning, not a quirk

How it fails

  • Failed-open trap venting steam continuously
  • Stall at low load → hammer → damaged HX and piping
BO-5

Combustion air, the HV / MAU interlock & the flue

Components

burner linkage or fully-metered controlO₂ trimflue / breechingdraft hood or barometric damperHV or MAU serving the boiler roomcombustion-air louver + end switchair-proving / differential pressure switchroom pressure sensorCO monitor

Controls & sequences

  • Combustion needs excess air — too little and you make CO and soot; too much and you send heat up the stack. It is measured, not guessed. An annual combustion analysis sets it: O₂ percentage, CO in ppm, stack temperature, efficiency
  • Where the air comes from decides the interlock. A sealed-combustion condensing boiler ducts its own outside air directly to the burner — it does not care about the room. An older atmospheric or low-efficiency boiler draws combustion air from the room, and the room has to be fed
  • That is what the HV or MAU is for. It tempers and delivers outside air to the boiler room, and the boiler is interlocked to it: fan status proven, discharge damper proven open. If the air unit is down, the boilers are held off. Deliberately
  • Room pressure is the hazard nobody sees. Exhaust fans, a dirty louver, or an MAU that is off can pull the boiler room negative. Flue gas then spills backwards out of the draft hood or barometric damper instead of going up the stack — into a room people walk into. That is a CO event, and it is silent
  • So the interlock protects people, not equipment. Never jumper an MAU proof to get heat back on a cold night
  • Rising stack temperature at the same firing rate means fouled tubes: heat going up the chimney instead of into the water

Operator tasks

  • Know, for each boiler, whether its combustion air is ducted, louvered, or delivered by an HV/MAU — and what proves it
  • Check that the HV/MAU schedule and the boiler schedule actually agree, including holidays and setback
  • Verify the CO monitor is alive, not merely installed; know where its alarm annunciates
  • Keep louvers and screens clear — check before heating season, and after any snow
  • Annual combustion test, recorded and trended year on year

How it fails

  • MAU off while boilers fire → room goes negative → flue gas spills at the draft hood → CO
  • MAU proof jumpered out during a service call and left that way
  • Blocked louver or a screen packed with snow
  • Sooted tubes → stack temperature climbing, efficiency falling
  • Linkage drifted after a repair, never re-tested
BO-6

Boiler safeties — hardwired, manual reset

Components

low-water cutoff (primary + auxiliary)water column / gauge glasshigh-limit aquastat (manual reset)operating limithigh & low gas pressure switchesflame safeguardsafety relief valve

Controls & sequences

  • The low-water cutoff stops the burner before the boiler fires dry. Most codes want a primary and an auxiliary. Probe types and float types are tested differently — know which you have
  • The high-limit is a separate device from the operating control, and it is manual reset on purpose: somebody must come and find out why the operating control failed
  • The safety relief valve is the last line, and it is purely mechanical. Its discharge must be piped full-size to a safe location, and never valved, plugged, or reduced
  • All of these are hardwired. The BMS is informed. It is never asked for permission

Operator tasks

  • Blow down the low-water cutoff on the schedule and log it
  • Test the high-limit; know where every reset button is
  • Verify the relief discharge is unobstructed and pointed somewhere safe
  • Never jumper a safety to keep heat on — call it in

How it fails

  • LWCO jumpered during a service call and left that way
  • Relief discharge piped into a corner where somebody stands
  • Repeated high-limit resets, cause never investigated

BM-2

Points — hardware, software, network

Components

AI · AO · BI · BO (hardware)AV · BV (software)mapped BACnet / Modbus objects (network)current switchauxiliary contactend switch / position feedbacksensor + transmitteractuator + linkage

Controls & sequences

  • The kind of point tells you where to go and fix it. A bad hardware point is a trip to the field — sensor, wire, terminal, transmitter, calibration, linkage. A bad software point is a trip to the logic; nothing in the field will help. A bad network point is the integration: gateway, register map, address, or the third-party device
  • Hardware points consume I/O and drive controller count and cost. Software points are effectively free, which is why there are so many
  • A software point cannot fail on its own, but its source can — a calculated mixed-air temperature will display a confident number long after one of its two sensors dies
  • Command is what the BMS asked for; status is proof from an independent device — only if that status is a hardware point
  • The status echo: some graphics show a software point that mirrors the command. It turns green because the BMS asked, not because anything happened, and it can never disagree. If command and status have never once differed, look for a wire
  • A current switch may prove the VFD is energized, not that the shaft turns. An aux contact proves the starter pulled in, not that the coupling is intact
  • Sensor drift produces confident, plausible, wrong numbers — the loop obediently controls to them
  • Fail position: spring-return actuators go to a known place on power loss. OA dampers usually fail closed
  • Present_Value is a BACnet property on every object. An AO’s Present_Value is its command. Do not confuse it with the loop’s process variable

Operator tasks

  • For every status on a graphic, know whether there is a wire behind it
  • Verify a sensor against a calibrated instrument at least annually
  • Stroke an actuator and watch the blade or stem, not the actuator
  • Compare command, status, and hardware — three layers that can all disagree

How it fails

  • A status that is a software echo — it can never reveal a failure
  • Loose linkage: the actuator strokes, the damper never moves
  • A drifted sensor that never alarms
  • A calculated point still reporting after its source died
BM-3

Loops & tuning

Components

PID blockgain / proportional bandintegral (reset) timederivativedeadbandoutput span

Controls & sequences

  • P reacts to the size of the error, I to how long it has persisted, D to how fast it changes. Most HVAC loops use P and I
  • Hunting is a tuning fault: too much gain, or integral wind-up. It wears actuators and burns energy
  • Saturation — a modulating output pinned at 100% or 0% — means capacity, a lying sensor, or an unachievable setpoint. It rarely alarms
  • Cascade: a slow loop’s output becomes a fast loop’s setpoint

Operator tasks

  • Trend the output, not just the process variable — the output tells you what the loop is doing
  • Change one parameter at a time and watch a full cycle

How it fails

  • A saturated valve that has been 100% open for weeks
  • Integral wind-up after a long off period
BM-4

Schedules, setpoints & reset strategies

Components

occupancy schedulesholiday calendaroptimal startsetbackOA resetDP / static resettrim & respond logic

Controls & sequences

  • Optimal start begins early enough to hit setpoint at occupancy — and no earlier
  • Reset schedules slide a setpoint with a measured condition: hot water hotter as it gets colder; DP setpoint down when valves are mostly closed
  • Trim & respond lowers a setpoint until a zone complains, then trims back. The biggest single energy lever most buildings have
  • Lab setback: unoccupied ACH reduction, and the interlocks that must survive it

Operator tasks

  • Check occupancy before chasing a valve — half of all 7am complaints end there
  • Verify the holiday calendar every year

How it fails

  • A schedule left in permanent occupied after a commissioning visit
  • A reset setpoint that never actually resets
BM-5

Trends & alarm management

Components

trend logssample interval vs COV24h · 7-day · seasonal windowsalarm classes & prioritiesalarm limits & delaysnotification routingexport / screenshot for the work order

Controls & sequences

  • A trend is the building’s memory. Trend before you touch — a setpoint changed blind fixes one screen and breaks another
  • You cannot trend the past. If a point was never logged that history does not exist and never will. Often the first action on an unexplained problem is to set the trend up, then wait
  • Overlay four points, always: process variable · setpoint · output % · command and status. The gap between PV and setpoint is the problem; the output reveals saturation; command and status say whether it was even running
  • Sample interval matters: a five-minute log will not show a valve hunting on a thirty-second cycle. COV logs on movement instead
  • Read the shape: flat = stale point · sawtooth = hunting · pinned at 100%/0% = saturation · daily steps = a schedule, not a fault · a step with no schedule = somebody did something, check the audit log
  • Alarm fatigue is a safety problem, not an annoyance. When everything alarms, nothing does
  • Fixing a nuisance alarm means fixing the sensor, the limit, or the real fault — never disabling the alarm

Operator tasks

  • Set trends before you need them
  • Pull a 7-day overlay before touching any loop — the weekend is the cleanest test of a schedule
  • Attach the trend to the work order; a trend is evidence
  • Review the alarm log weekly and drive the nuisance count down

How it fails

  • Alarm limits widened to silence the log
  • Trend intervals so long the event never appears
  • A flat line mistaken for good control
BM-6

Networks, protocols & integration

Components

Controls & sequences

  • MS/TP is a twisted-pair daisy chain: termination, polarity, and matching baud rate all matter
  • A whole branch of the graphic going stale at once points at a bus, not at a device
  • Chillers and meters often arrive on Modbus behind a gateway — the point list is only as good as the mapping
  • Comm loss should cost you visibility, not control. If it stops equipment, something is wired wrong

Operator tasks

  • Know which controller sits on which bus
  • Never add a device to a live bus without checking address and baud

How it fails

  • Duplicate MAC address takes a whole trunk down
  • Missing termination resistor — works in summer, fails in winter
BM-7

Overrides, interlocks & hardwired safeties

Components

freezestathigh-static cutoutlow-limit switchproof-of-flow switchsmoke detector shutdownEPO

Controls & sequences

  • A hardwired safety acts whether or not the BMS agrees. That is the point of it
  • An interlock is a condition, not a suggestion. If a chiller will not start, the interlock is telling you something true — satisfy it, do not defeat it
  • Before any override: is the PV really failing its setpoint · is the output saturated · does status agree with command, and hardware with status · is an interlock holding it off · when will I remove this?
  • The FACP is stand-alone. It commands dampers, fans, and elevators directly; the BMS may only monitor

  • Write down every override with a date and an owner
  • Never troubleshoot a life-safety device from a BMS graphic

  • A freezestat jumpered out “temporarily”

  • Keep the as-built points list current; it decays fastest of all documents

How it fails

  • Sequences written for a design that was value-engineered away
  • Nobody has read the SOO since turnover

  • Ten reasons the set may be running — and in six of them the building is completely normal
  • Real events: a utility outage, ATS on Emergency · a utility anomaly — brownout, single-phasing, frequency excursion
  • Scheduled and planned: the weekly no-load exercise, ATS stays on Normal · a monthly loaded test · an annual load bank test · a cool-down after retransfer, engine unloaded with the timer counting · a manual start at the controller · the ATS test switch simulating an outage · peak shave or demand response, if the site is enrolled
  • Abnormal — investigate: a sensing fault. The utility is fine, but a drifted voltage relay, a loose neutral, or a failed phase monitor has convinced the ATS otherwise
  • Before you stop it, read five things in order: the ATS — read at the switch itself: controller screen · Source Available and Load On lamps · the Not-In-Auto lamp · the mechanical position indicator · bypass and isolation handles if it is a bypass-isolation unit, all from the front with the door closed → the utility (volts on all three legs?) → who started it, and was it you? → the controller mode (AUTO · exercising · cooling down · not in AUTO) → the schedule and the work orders
  • The person who starts a generator is the person who stops it. If you did not start it, you do not stop it — go and find whoever did: the technician, the vendor, the on-call engineer, the supervisor who authorized the test. That machine is running because somebody put it there for a reason, and you do not yet know the reason
  • The backwards inference: during a no-load exercise the building is perfectly normal — that is what the test is. “Everything looks fine, so the generator should not be running” is exactly the reasoning that shuts a generator down mid-test

Operator tasks

  • The person who starts a generator is the person who stops it — if you did not start it, find who did
  • Know where the ATS position indicator is in every building, before you need it
  • Post the exercise schedule where the engine is, not only in the BMS
  • Log every generator run: what started it, ATS position, duration, and who witnessed it
  • You stop a running generator for one reason only — a genuine emergency in front of you: fire, mechanical failure, someone injured. Then you are choosing to drop emergency power, knowingly

How it fails

  • Stopped during an outage → egress lighting, fire alarm, fire pump, elevator recall and lab exhaust all drop; containment is lost
  • Stopped during cool-down → oil cokes in the turbo bearing. It does not fail today. It fails on the night you need it
  • E-stop pressed → the set is in lockout and needs a manual reset. The generator that merely looked wrong is now genuinely unavailable
  • A sensing fault left uninvestigated because somebody kept resetting it
  • Stopped by somebody who did not start it — the test, the vendor visit, or the outage response all end at once, and nobody knows why
Structure per module: components → controls & sequences → operator tasks → how it fails. The failure column is deliberate: a new hire remembers a system by the way it breaks.
